AI in Design

What AI Still Can't Do in Design (And Why It Matters)

Sep 3, 2026 · 14 min read

The gap between the demo and the deadline

Every few weeks another AI design tool posts a demo that looks like magic. A prompt goes in, a polished screen comes out. Type "fintech onboarding flow, dark mode, minimal" into Galileo AI or Uizard and you get something that looks presentable in under a minute. I use these tools myself, regularly, and I'm not writing this to dismiss them. But there's a gap between what a demo shows and what a real project needs, and that gap is where most of my actual work happens.

Clients who follow AI design news often arrive at a call with a specific question hiding underneath the polite one. The polite question is "what do you think about using AI in the process?" The real question is "can I skip paying for a chunk of this?" It's a fair thing to wonder. Some of the work genuinely can be compressed now. Wireframe exploration, copy variants, icon sets, even rough component libraries — a year ago that was hours of manual labor, now it's minutes of prompting and curating. That's real and I'd be lying if I said otherwise.

But the parts of a project that actually determine whether a product ships and performs — the parts clients are paying for when they hire a designer instead of generating screens themselves — are almost untouched by any of this. This article is about naming those parts specifically, not in a defensive "AI will never replace human creativity" way, but in a practical way you can use to decide what to automate and what to still budget a real person's time for.

AI doesn't know your business, and it can't ask

Every design decision worth making is downstream of a business constraint that isn't written anywhere an AI model can read. A generative tool can produce a checkout flow that follows every UX best practice in its training data. It cannot know that your payment provider takes 11 seconds to return a webhook confirmation, so the "processing" state needs to be designed for a much longer wait than a textbook checkout would assume. It cannot know that your support team is three people and every extra settings screen you ship generates two more tickets a week. It cannot know that the stakeholder who signs off on this flow was burned by a redesign eighteen months ago and now reflexively vetoes anything that looks "too different."

None of that is written in a brief. It comes out over the course of conversations, in the fifteen minutes after the official meeting ends, in a Slack message sent at 11pm, in the way a founder's face changes when you show a version that's technically better but emotionally wrong for the brand they've spent five years building. This is not mystical "human intuition" — it's just accumulated, unstructured context that lives in people's heads and relationships, and no model has access to it because it was never written down for anyone to train on.

I've started to think of this as the actual product of design work: not the screens, but the translation of messy, half-spoken business reality into a coherent set of decisions. AI is extremely good at the last mile of that process — turning a decision into pixels — and completely absent from the first ninety percent, which is figuring out what the decision should be. If you hand an AI tool a brief that's missing that context, it will confidently produce something plausible and wrong, and the danger is that "plausible" is exactly convincing enough to get approved by someone who doesn't know what questions to ask.

AI can't run a discovery process, only fill out one

Discovery is not a form you fill in. It's a series of conversations that change direction based on what the last answer revealed. A good discovery session with a client goes something like this: you ask why they want a dashboard, they say "so users can see their data," you ask what decision users make after seeing that data, and the client pauses, because they've actually never thought about it that specifically. That pause is the most valuable moment in the entire engagement. It's where the real brief gets written, usually contradicting the one they sent you originally.

AI tools are excellent at generating discovery artifacts — personas, competitor summaries, feature matrices, even plausible-sounding user interview questions. What they can't do is notice that a stakeholder's answer contradicts something they said ten minutes earlier, and use that contradiction to open up the real conversation. They can't read the room when a product owner and a marketing lead disagree on what "success" means for a feature, and gently surface that disagreement before it becomes a fight three sprints into development. A transcript summarizer can tell you what was said in a user interview. It won't tell you that the user's tone changed the moment you asked about pricing, or that they answered a completely different question than the one you asked because the real question made them uncomfortable.

I ran a discovery phase last year for a B2B logistics platform where the client insisted, in writing, three separate times, that their priority was "a modern, clean UI." Two workshops in, it became clear the actual problem was that dispatchers were re-entering the same data into four different screens because none of the existing tools talked to each other. The UI wasn't ugly. It was fine. What they needed was information architecture that eliminated redundant entry, and the "modern, clean UI" request was a proxy for "this feels broken and I don't have the vocabulary to explain why." No prompt would have surfaced that. It took two in-person sessions and a lot of patient, slightly uncomfortable questioning.

If you're evaluating whether to shortcut discovery with AI-generated research summaries, the honest test is this: could the summary contradict itself in a way that reveals the real problem? If a tool only ever produces internally consistent, tidy documents, it's not doing discovery — it's doing paperwork that looks like discovery, and paperwork doesn't catch the dispatcher problem.

AI can't sit in the room when trade-offs get argued

Every real project eventually hits a moment where two legitimate goals conflict and someone has to choose. Should the onboarding flow ask for payment info before or after the user sees value? Should the settings page prioritize power users or the 80% who never touch advanced options? Should the brand's signature bright color be used on a call-to-action even though it fails contrast guidelines at that exact shade? These aren't design problems with a single correct answer sitting in a dataset. They're negotiations between departments with different incentives — growth wants the first, support wants the second, brand wants the third — and the designer's actual job in that room is not "know the right answer" but broker a decision that the whole team will actually commit to and not quietly resent later.

An AI tool can generate options. It can even generate reasonable-sounding arguments for each option, if you prompt it that way. What it cannot do is sense that the growth lead just got a rough number from the board meeting and is negotiating from a place of fear rather than data, or that the support lead's objection two months ago about a similar issue never got properly addressed and is resurfacing here in disguise. It can't build the political capital that gets a genuinely better but unpopular decision through a room. That capital is built over months of being right in small, verifiable ways, and it's one of the actual reasons clients keep paying an outside designer instead of routing every decision through an internal AI-assisted workflow: someone credible needs to own the recommendation and be willing to defend it in a meeting where feelings are involved.

"We didn't need someone to make more options. We needed someone to make the call and explain why, in a way the sales team would accept without a three-week argument."

That's close to a direct quote from a product lead I worked with on a SaaS redesign, and it's the clearest articulation I've heard of what clients are actually buying when they hire a designer for anything beyond production work. Generating options is now cheap. Owning a trade-off and defending it under pressure is not, and I don't see a credible path to that changing soon, because it's not a data problem — it's a trust and accountability problem.

AI can't build a design system that survives contact with a real product

Design systems look, from the outside, like exactly the kind of structured, rules-based problem AI should be great at: define tokens, define components, generate variants, done. And tools have gotten genuinely useful at the mechanical parts — generating a spacing scale, producing color ramps with accessible contrast built in, scaffolding component states you'd otherwise build by hand. I use AI for exactly this on almost every project now and it saves real hours.

Where it falls apart is governance, which is the actual hard part of a design system and the part nobody puts in the demo video. A design system isn't a component library, it's a set of decisions about what happens when reality doesn't fit the system. What do you do when a specific product team needs a table with sticky columns and your system's table component wasn't built for that? Do you extend the system, fork the component, or push back on the request? Who has authority to make that call, and how do you keep forty designers and developers across six teams from quietly diverging anyway, three sprints later, because the sanctioned answer took too long to arrive?

That's an organizational design problem wearing a UI costume. It requires knowing which team ignores documentation, which engineer will build a one-off component out of frustration if a ticket sits too long, which stakeholder will use "the system doesn't support this" as an excuse to derail a launch they were never enthusiastic about. AI can maintain a beautifully documented Figma library. It cannot maintain the social contract that keeps forty people actually using it instead of working around it. I've seen design systems fail — expensively, after months of investment — not because the components were badly built but because nobody owned the adoption problem, and that's a people problem no amount of better tooling fixes.

If you want a deeper look at how this plays out specifically in Figma-based workflows, I've written before about why most design systems die from adoption, not from bad architecture, and the pattern holds regardless of how the components were generated.

AI can't be held accountable, and that changes what it's allowed to decide

This is the least discussed limitation and probably the most important one for anyone making a hiring or process decision right now. When a designer signs off on a flow and it ships with a usability problem that costs the client money, there's a person to have that conversation with. They can explain their reasoning, they can be wrong in a way that's investigated and learned from, they can lose the client's trust and that has consequences that shape how carefully they work next time. Accountability isn't a punishment mechanism, it's an information system — it's how organizations learn what went wrong and adjust who gets to make which calls in the future.

AI-generated design decisions don't carry that signal. If a generated flow causes a drop in conversion, there's no one to sit down with and ask "what were you optimizing for when you made this choice?" because there was no choice in the accountable sense, there was a statistically likely output based on patterns in training data. That's fine for low-stakes, easily reversible decisions — a button color, an icon style, a placeholder illustration. It's a serious liability for decisions that are expensive to reverse: information architecture that shapes how an entire product is understood, a permissions model in an enterprise tool, a health or financial app's error states where getting it wrong has real consequences for a real person on the other end.

This is also why "AI-assisted" and "AI-owned" need to stay separate categories in how you brief and budget a project. It's completely reasonable to have AI generate ten variations of an empty state illustration and have a human pick and refine the best one — the human retains ownership of the decision even though the labor was outsourced. It's a different thing entirely to let a generated flow go to production without a person who can explain and defend every meaningful choice in it, because when — not if — something in it turns out to be wrong, someone needs to be able to explain why it happened and what changes as a result. Clients who skip that step aren't saving money, they're deferring a cost and removing the mechanism that would have caught it early.

Where AI genuinely earns its place in the process

None of this is an argument for ignoring these tools, and I'd be doing my own clients a disservice if I pretended otherwise. The honest way to think about it is that AI has gotten very good at collapsing the distance between an idea and a visible draft of that idea, and that's genuinely valuable — it just isn't the part of the job that was ever the bottleneck for a competent designer. The bottleneck was never "how fast can I make a screen." It was always "how do I know this is the right screen." Here's roughly how I actually use these tools day to day, and where I've deliberately drawn a line:

  • Divergent exploration, not final decisions. I'll generate a dozen layout directions for a landing page in minutes to get past the blank-canvas problem, then throw out most of them once I understand the client's actual constraints better. The output is a starting point for judgment, not a substitute for it.
  • Production speed on well-defined problems. Once a pattern is approved — a card component, a form state, an empty state — AI-assisted tools inside Figma and code editors cut real production time on variants and responsive states. This is where the ROI is most obvious and least controversial.
  • First-draft copy and content structure. Getting from nothing to a rough draft of onboarding copy or error messages is faster with AI, but every string still gets edited against the actual voice and the actual failure modes of the product, because generic copy in an error state is where trust quietly leaks out of a product.
  • Research synthesis, with a human check on contradictions. Summarizing ten user interviews is a genuinely useful AI task. I still read the raw transcripts myself first, because the summary smooths over exactly the contradictions and hesitations that usually contain the real insight.
  • Accessibility and contrast checking as a baseline, not a finish line. Automated checks catch the mechanical failures reliably. They don't catch a screen reader experience that's technically compliant and still confusing, which still needs a person to actually use it that way.

The pattern across all of these is that AI is excellent inside a decision that's already been made, and unreliable at making the decision itself. That distinction is the whole article, really. Every genuinely useful application above is downstream of a choice a person made about direction, tone, priority, or risk. Take away the person making that choice and the tool doesn't fail loudly — it fails quietly, by producing something plausible that nobody actually evaluated against the real constraints of the business, and plausible-but-wrong is far more expensive to catch after launch than before it.

For agencies and studios building AI into their process, the practical takeaway is to be explicit, in the proposal and the timeline, about which deliverables are AI-accelerated production and which are still fundamentally human judgment calls — and to price and staff those differently, rather than letting "we use AI now" quietly become an excuse to under-resource the judgment side. For clients evaluating a designer or studio, the useful question isn't "do you use AI tools" — almost everyone competent does, by now. It's "walk me through a decision on a recent project where the AI-generated option was wrong, and how you caught it." If they don't have a specific answer, they either aren't using these tools seriously or aren't checking their output seriously, and either one should worry you more than a slower, more traditional process would.

I wrote more about how this shift is reshaping pricing conversations with clients in a separate piece on what to actually charge for once AI compresses production time, because the tooling question and the pricing question turn out to be the same question in disguise: you're not paying for screens anymore, you never really were, you're paying for someone to be right about what the screens should do and to be accountable when they're not.
