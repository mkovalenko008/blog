AI Illustrations for a Landing Page in One Hour: Step-by-Step

Sep 19, 2026 · 20 min read

Why this workflow exists

Somewhere around 2023, "AI illustrations" became a synonym for "cheap and generic" in a lot of design circles, and honestly, most of the reputation is earned. You've seen the landing pages: a floating gradient blob, a person gesturing at a laptop with six fingers quietly cropped out of frame, a color palette that looks like it was chosen by committee inside the model itself. That's not a tooling problem. That's a process problem. People type one prompt, take the first image, drop it into Figma, and ship it.

I've used AI illustration generation on real client landing pages — SaaS onboarding flows, a fintech waitlist page, a couple of DTC product launches — and the thing that made the output usable wasn't a magic prompt. It was treating the generator like a tool in a pipeline, not the whole pipeline. That distinction is what separates "AI slop" from illustrations a client will actually approve without three rounds of "can we make it less AI-looking."

This article is a literal walkthrough of how I get a full set of custom illustrations — hero image, three to five supporting icons or scene illustrations, consistent style across all of them — ready for a landing page in about an hour. Not a mood board. Not "AI-assisted ideation." Production-ready assets, exported, optimized, and placed in the layout. I'll give you the timing breakdown, the actual prompt structure I use, the tools, and the parts where things go wrong if you skip a step.

One caveat up front: this works best for landing pages where illustration is decorative and supportive — hero sections, feature call-outs, empty states, onboarding screens — not for brand-defining mascot work or anything that needs to survive five years of reuse across a full design system. For that, you still want a human illustrator building something proprietary. This is for the 80% of landing page illustration work that's currently either "stock illustration site" or "nothing, because there wasn't budget or time."

The 10-minute setup that determines everything

The single biggest mistake I see designers make with AI illustration tools is opening the generator first. Don't. The first ten minutes of this process happen entirely away from the AI tool, and they're what make the next fifty minutes fast instead of a frustrating loop of regenerating random variations.

Step 1: Lock the style reference before you touch a prompt box

Every AI image generator has a tendency to default to its "house style" unless you constrain it hard. Midjourney defaults toward glossy, dramatic lighting. DALL-E 3 (via ChatGPT) defaults toward flat, slightly cartoonish corporate-safe illustration. Ideogram and Recraft lean toward cleaner vector-like outputs. If you don't pick a direction, you'll get the tool's default, and the tool's default is what everyone else's landing page also has.

So step one: find or sketch a reference. I keep a swipe file of illustration styles organized by mood — "flat geometric with two-tone shadow," "risograph texture with grain," "linework with single accent color," "isometric with soft ambient occlusion." Pick one that matches the brand's existing visual language. If the brand already has a logo with rounded geometric shapes and a specific two-color palette, that's your constraint. Don't introduce a third style into the page.

This takes two minutes if you already keep a swipe file (you should), and five minutes if you're starting cold and need to search for three or four reference images.

Step 2: Write the style descriptor once, reuse it everywhere

Here's the actual trick that saves you the most time in this entire process: write a single, reusable style descriptor — a chunk of prompt language that describes the rendering style, not the subject — and paste it into every single prompt for this project. Something like:

flat vector illustration, two-tone color palette of deep navy and warm coral, thick uniform line weight, no gradients, generous negative space, minimal shading, geometric simplified shapes, subtle grain texture overlay

Every image you generate for this landing page starts with that block, then adds the subject. This is what creates consistency across five different illustrations without needing five different fine-tuned models. It's the AI-illustration equivalent of a design system's component library — you're defining the tokens once and reusing them.

Write this descriptor by actually looking at your reference images and describing them in plain, specific language — line weight, palette (name actual colors or hex-adjacent descriptions, not "vibrant colors"), whether there's texture or grain, whether shadows exist, how simplified the shapes are, whether it's flat or has depth. Vague adjectives like "modern" or "clean" do almost nothing; models have seen those words attached to a million different visual outputs and they average out to nothing distinctive.

Step 3: List your shot list before generating anything

Two more minutes: write down exactly what illustrations the page needs. Not "a few illustrations," an actual list. For a typical SaaS landing page that's usually:

  • One hero illustration or scene (the big one, above the fold)
  • Three to four feature-section illustrations (one per feature block)
  • Optionally, one empty-state or CTA-section illustration

Knowing this before you start means you're not discovering scope mid-generation, which is where the hour balloons into three hours. You also want to note the aspect ratio each slot needs — hero images are often wide (16:9 or wider), feature icons are often square or 4:3. Generating at the wrong ratio and cropping later is how you lose the parts of the composition you actually wanted.

Minutes 10–40: generating and iterating with intent

This is the bulk of the hour, but it moves fast because of the setup work. I'm going to walk through this using a composite example: a landing page for a fictional habit-tracking app called Loomly, with a navy-and-coral flat illustration style, needing a hero scene and four feature illustrations.

Generate the hero first, and generate it in batches, not one at a time

Start with the hardest, highest-visibility asset: the hero illustration. This is the one people will actually look at, so it deserves the most iteration budget. My prompt for the hero looks like the style descriptor plus a specific, concrete scene description — not "a person using an app," but something with actual staging:

flat vector illustration, two-tone color palette of deep navy and warm coral, thick uniform line weight, no gradients, generous negative space, minimal shading, geometric simplified shapes, subtle grain texture overlay — a person sitting cross-legged on the floor checking off items on a large floating checklist card, small plant and coffee mug beside them, morning light suggested through simple shapes, wide horizontal composition with empty space on the left third for text overlay

Notice the last clause: "empty space on the left third for text overlay." This is something almost nobody includes in AI illustration prompts and it's one of the highest-leverage additions you can make. Landing page heroes almost always need headline copy overlaid on or next to the image. If you don't ask for compositional breathing room, you'll get a centered, symmetrical illustration that fights with your headline for the same visual real estate, and you'll spend twenty minutes in Figma trying to make text legible over a busy area of the image.

Generate four variations at once (every major tool supports this — Midjourney gives you four by default, DALL-E through ChatGPT gives you one but you can ask for multiple in sequence, Ideogram and Recraft give you four). Don't evaluate them individually and regenerate one at a time; look at the batch as a set and identify what's actually wrong versus what's just not your favorite. There's a difference between "the anatomy is broken" (regenerate) and "I like the composition of image 2 but the color balance of image 4" (that's a note for your next prompt revision, not a reason to throw everything out).

Iterate on the prompt, not just the seed

If your first batch is close but not right, resist the urge to just hit "regenerate" repeatedly hoping for a better roll. Change something specific in the prompt language instead. If the composition is too centered, add "asymmetrical composition, subject weighted to the right side of frame." If the color balance feels off, be more explicit: "navy as the dominant color covering roughly 70% of the illustration, coral used only as a small accent on 2–3 elements." Models respond much better to added specificity than to random rerolls.

This is the point where a lot of the "AI slop" reputation actually comes from — people treat generation as a slot machine instead of a conversation. You get better results faster by making one deliberate change per iteration and observing what it does, the same way you'd adjust a single variable in any other design tool.

Budget about 10 minutes for the hero: two to three rounds of four images each, landing on one or two finalists.

Generate the feature illustrations as a set, referencing the hero

Now the four feature illustrations. This is where consistency either holds together or falls apart, and it's also where you can work fastest if you set it up right.

Use the exact same style descriptor block, unchanged, for every one of these. Do not rewrite it, do not "improve" it between images — consistency comes from repetition, not refinement. Then vary only the subject clause:

  • "a hand placing a checkmark into a simple progress bar shaped like a plant growing"
  • "a phone screen showing a streak calendar with small flame icons on completed days, simplified isometric perspective"
  • "two overlapping speech bubbles with a simple heart icon between them, suggesting community encouragement"
  • "a simplified trophy shape sitting on a pedestal made of stacked geometric blocks"

If your tool supports a reference image or style-reference parameter (Midjourney's `--sref`, or uploading the hero image as a style reference in tools that support image-prompting), use it. This locks in line weight, palette, and rendering style much more reliably than text description alone, especially across four separate generation sessions. In Midjourney specifically, I'll generate the hero, then append `--sref` with the hero's image URL to every subsequent prompt in the same session. That single move does more for visual consistency than any amount of extra descriptive text.

Budget about 15–20 minutes for four feature illustrations — roughly four minutes each, since the style is already dialed in and you're mostly iterating on subject clarity, not style-fixing.

Don't skip the "does this actually communicate" check

Before moving to cleanup, look at each finalist and ask: if someone saw only this illustration, with no caption, would they have a rough guess what it's about? AI illustrations fail landing pages most often not because they look "AI-generated" but because they're pretty and meaningless — a nice abstract shape composition that doesn't reinforce the feature copy next to it. A streak calendar with flame icons reads instantly. A vague swirl of navy shapes next to the words "Build lasting habits" reads as decoration, not communication. Spend the last two minutes of this phase sanity-checking legibility of concept, not just aesthetics.

Minutes 40–50: cleanup and making it look intentional

Raw AI output, even good raw AI output, almost never goes straight to production. This ten-minute phase is what separates "obviously AI, obviously rushed" from "custom illustration," and it's mostly small, fast fixes.

Fix the background first

Most generators give you a background — sometimes a subtle texture, sometimes a full scene, sometimes an ugly gradient you didn't ask for. For landing page use, you almost always want your illustration on a transparent or flat background so it composites into your existing section background rather than sitting in its own rectangle. Tools like Adobe Express, remove.bg, or even Photoshop's subject-select-and-mask (if you have it) handle this in under a minute per image. Some generators — Recraft notably — let you generate directly with a transparent background as an output option, which skips this step entirely; if your tool supports that, use it and save yourself five images' worth of masking.

Correct the obvious anatomy and object errors

Even good outputs usually have one small local error: a hand with an extra finger, an object that doesn't quite connect to another object, a line that terminates in the wrong place. Because this style is flat and simplified rather than photorealistic, these errors are much easier to fix than they'd be in a photorealistic generation — you're not retouching skin texture, you're fixing a simple shape. Generative fill in Photoshop, or the inpainting tools built into Midjourney (vary region) and DALL-E, handle 90% of these fixes in thirty seconds each. For genuinely simple flat-shape errors, sometimes it's faster to just open the file in Illustrator or Figma and manually redraw the one broken shape — a five-second fix with the pen tool is sometimes quicker than three inpainting attempts.

Unify color values across the set

Even with a locked style descriptor, you'll often find your "navy" drifted slightly warmer in one image and cooler in another, because the model isn't literally reading hex codes — it's interpreting language. Before finalizing, drop all your chosen illustrations into one Figma frame side by side and eyeball them together. If one is noticeably off, use a Hue/Saturation or Selective Color adjustment (in Photoshop, or Figma's color adjustment on the layer if you're working with a flattened raster) to nudge it back in line. This is a two-minute pass across the whole set, not a per-image ordeal, and it's the single most effective step for making five separately-generated images read as "one illustration set" instead of "five illustrations that happen to share two colors."

Add or restore the human touches that sell "intentional"

A few small manual additions go a long way toward making AI illustration read as deliberate design work rather than a raw export: a consistent drop shadow treatment applied uniformly across all images (not whatever ambient shadow the model happened to render), a subtle noise/grain overlay applied as one layer over the whole set (bonus: this also helps hide small compression or generation artifacts), and cropping every image to consistent padding within its frame so nothing looks randomly zoomed compared to its neighbors. None of this requires illustration skill — it requires the same finishing instincts you'd apply to any other visual asset before shipping it.

Minutes 50–60: export, optimize, and place it in the page

This is the step people rush or skip, and it's why AI illustrations sometimes tank Lighthouse scores or look blurry on retina displays despite looking fine in the generator's preview window.

Export at the right resolution, not the max resolution

Most generators output somewhere between 1024px and 2048px on the long edge by default, with upscaling options going higher. Figure out your actual display size on the landing page first — if your hero illustration displays at 600px wide in the layout, you need roughly 1200px wide for a clean 2x retina export, not 4096px "because bigger is safer." Oversized exports bloat page weight for zero visual benefit past the 2x threshold, and landing page load time directly affects conversion, which is presumably the entire point of the page you're building.

Convert to the right file format for the job

If your illustration is genuinely flat vector-style output with solid color fills and clean edges, it's often worth vectorizing it — Illustrator's Image Trace, or Recraft's built-in "convert to SVG" feature, does this well for simplified geometric styles. An SVG scales infinitely, weighs a fraction of a raster export, and can be manipulated with CSS if you want subtle hover states or animated details later. If the illustration has grain texture, complex shading, or gradient work that won't vectorize cleanly, export as WebP instead of PNG — WebP typically gives you 25–35% smaller file sizes than PNG at visually identical quality, and every browser you need to support in 2024 onward handles it fine, with PNG as a simple fallback if you're being extra cautious about older embedded browsers.

Place with the same care you'd give a custom illustration

Drop each asset into its actual layout position, not just onto the canvas. Check the hero illustration against the actual headline and CTA button text at the actual breakpoint — this is when you find out whether that "empty space on the left third" request from earlier actually left enough room, or whether the illustration's edge clips awkwardly against your button on mobile. Check contrast: if the illustration sits directly on a background color close to one of its own dominant colors, it can visually merge into the section rather than pop as a focal element. A one-line adjustment — nudging the section background half a shade lighter or darker, or adding a very subtle contained shape behind the illustration — often fixes this in under a minute.

Finally, check responsive behavior. Illustrations with a lot of horizontal negative space (which you asked for, for the text-overlay reason above) can look oddly empty when the layout stacks the image above the text on mobile instead of beside it. Sometimes the fix is a second, tighter crop of the same illustration for mobile rather than forcing one crop to serve both breakpoints — since you already have the source file, this costs you thirty seconds, not a redo.

What goes wrong when people skip steps

I want to be specific about failure modes here because "it looks AI-generated" is usually a symptom with a traceable cause, not a vague aesthetic judgment, and every cause maps to a step above that got skipped.

The mismatched-style problem

You've seen landing pages where the hero illustration is glossy and 3D-rendered but the feature icons below it are flat line art. This happens when someone generates each image independently, in separate sessions, without a locked style descriptor or reference image carrying through. The fix is entirely the setup phase — write the descriptor once, reuse it verbatim, use style-reference parameters where available. This is genuinely the single highest-impact fix in this entire workflow, and skipping it is the fastest way to make an otherwise good set of illustrations look cheap.

The "beautiful but meaningless" problem

Abstract blobs, generic gradients, floating geometric shapes with no relationship to the product. This happens when the prompt describes mood ("innovative, futuristic, exciting") instead of a concrete scene or object ("a hand placing a checkmark into a progress bar shaped like a plant"). Concrete nouns and verbs in your prompt produce concrete, legible illustrations. Adjectives produce vibes. Landing pages need the former; vibes belong in a mood board, not a feature section that's supposed to explain what your product does.

The uncanny-anatomy problem

Extra fingers, warped hands, faces with slightly wrong proportions. This is a known limitation, not a solved one, even in current-generation models — it's improved a lot but hasn't disappeared. The mitigation isn't hoping a future model fixes it; it's a deliberate style choice. Flat, simplified, geometric illustration styles are far more forgiving of this failure mode than semi-realistic ones, because a stylized hand with a slightly odd proportion reads as "stylistic choice," while a semi-realistic hand with the same error reads as "broken." If your brand can support a simplified illustration style — and most SaaS and DTC landing pages can — leaning into simplification isn't just faster to generate, it's structurally more resistant to the errors that make AI images look wrong.

The oversized-file problem

Four 4MB PNGs on a landing page will tank your Core Web Vitals and, by extension, probably your conversion rate and your SEO. This is purely a discipline problem in the last ten minutes — export at the size you need, compress, use WebP or SVG, and actually test the page's load performance before calling it done. It's the least glamorous part of the process and the part most likely to get skipped when a designer is happy with how the images look and eager to call the job finished.

The copyright and rights-clarity problem

Depending on which tool and plan you're using, commercial usage rights for AI-generated images vary — some platforms grant broad commercial rights on paid plans, some have more restrictive terms on free tiers, and the underlying legal landscape around AI training data is still being litigated in ways that could theoretically affect future usage rights. This isn't a design step, it's a client-communication step: before you ship AI-generated illustrations on a paying client's landing page, know what plan and license terms you generated them under, and be able to tell the client plainly what that means. It takes two minutes to check and it's the kind of thing that matters far more than it seems to in the moment you're excited about a good hero image.

When to skip this workflow entirely

I'd be doing you a disservice if I framed this as "AI illustration, always, for everything," because it isn't, and pretending otherwise is how the whole category earns its bad reputation with clients who've been burned by a rushed job.

Skip this workflow, or use it only for early exploration, when: the illustration needs to become a long-term brand asset — a mascot, a recurring character, something that'll appear across dozens of future touchpoints and needs a level of proprietary consistency and legal clarity that a from-scratch illustrator delivers better than repeated AI generation sessions. When the client's brand guidelines require pixel-precise, hand-crafted linework that a vector-trace of an AI raster won't cleanly replicate. When you're working in a regulated industry (health, finance, legal) where a client's legal or compliance team has specific, justified concerns about AI-generated visual content and where you don't have the bandwidth mid-project to research and resolve those concerns properly.

And skip it, honestly, when you don't actually need illustration at all — a well-shot product screenshot, a simple icon set from an established icon library, or just strong typography and whitespace often serves a landing page better than any illustration, AI-generated or otherwise. The existence of a fast illustration workflow is not a reason to add illustration to every section of every page; it's a reason to stop treating "we don't have budget for a custom illustrator" as an excuse for shipping nothing, or for shipping the same stock illustration everyone's design-system-in-a-box vendor is also shipping.

Where this workflow earns its place is the large middle ground: landing pages that need a handful of on-brand supporting visuals, on a timeline or budget that never had room for a commissioned illustrator, where the alternative was genuinely "generic stock art" or "blank space," not "a beautiful custom illustration we're cutting corners on." In that middle ground — which is most of the landing pages actually getting built and shipped this year — an hour of disciplined, structured work with an AI generator produces something a lot closer to custom illustration than most people, including most designers who've had a bad experience with these tools, currently expect.

The hour I've described isn't a shortcut around design judgment. It's design judgment applied at a different point in the process — before generation, in the style lock and shot list, and after generation, in the cleanup and integration — with the generation itself compressed down to the fifteen or twenty minutes it actually deserves. Treat the tool as a very fast, very literal junior illustrator who needs precise direction and a second pass on everything it hands you, and the hour holds up. Treat it as a vending machine for finished art, and you'll get exactly what everyone else got.
