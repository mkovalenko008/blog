How I Actually Use Midjourney on Client Projects

Oct 5, 2026 · 18 min read

Why a UI/UX Designer Even Touches Midjourney

I want to get one thing out of the way before anything else: I don't design interfaces in Midjourney. If you've seen the wave of "AI-generated app UI" posts floating around, where someone types "fintech dashboard, dark mode, glassmorphism, 8k, trending on Dribbble" and gets back a gorgeous but completely unusable image, you already know why. Those images look like interfaces the way a movie prop looks like a working telephone. They have buttons that don't align to any grid, text that's actual gibberish, and spacing that no real design system would ever produce. If a client asked me to build from one of those, I'd have to throw it away and start over anyway.

So why bother at all? Because the parts of my job that aren't pixel-perfect interface construction — the parts where I'm still figuring out what something should feel like before I know what it should look like — are exactly the parts Midjourney is good at. Mood, atmosphere, texture, color relationships, visual metaphor, the emotional register of a brand before it has a logo. That's a different skill than laying out a checkout flow, and it's a skill Midjourney has gotten disturbingly good at.

I've been using it on client work for a bit over two years now, across maybe thirty projects — mostly branding-adjacent UI work, marketing sites, a few product launches where the client wanted something that didn't look like every other SaaS template. In that time I've developed a pretty specific, pretty narrow use case for it. Not the one you see in the "10 ways to use AI in your design workflow" listicles. A narrower, more honest one. This is that breakdown.

Where It Actually Earns Its Keep

The single biggest win, by a wide margin, is early-stage mood exploration — the phase that used to mean three hours on Pinterest and a moodboard full of other people's finished work. Clients hire me partly because they can't articulate what they want. They say things like "modern but warm" or "premium but not corporate" and then look at me expectantly, as if those words mean something concrete. They don't, not yet. My job in week one is to turn vague feelings into specific visual decisions, and Midjourney lets me do that conversation in images instead of adjectives.

Here's a real example. A wellness-tech startup came to me wanting a brand refresh and a new marketing site. The founder kept saying "earthy but futuristic," which is the kind of phrase that means everything and nothing. Instead of pulling together a moodboard of other brands (which always risks anchoring the client on someone else's identity, and risks me accidentally recreating something copyrighted), I spent about forty minutes generating variations: warm clay tones against sterile lab whites, organic shapes intersecting with grid patterns, plant photography rendered with a slight synthetic sheen. None of these were usable assets. All of them were references I could put in front of the founder and say "closer to this, or closer to this?" We converged on a direction in one afternoon that would have taken three rounds of moodboard revisions the old way.

That's the actual value proposition: speed of visual conversation, not speed of production. I'm not shipping Midjourney output. I'm using it to compress the fuzziest, most subjective part of a project — the part where two humans are trying to agree on a feeling — into something we can point at and argue about concretely.

The second place it earns its keep is texture and material exploration for things that will eventually become real design assets, built properly, by me, in Figma. If I'm designing a hero section and I want a background that feels like frosted glass over a gradient mesh, or a texture that reads as "soft but technical," I'll generate a dozen options, pick the one that nails the feeling, and then recreate the effect natively — gradients, blend modes, actual noise textures licensed for commercial use — rather than ship the AI image itself. The Midjourney output becomes a very fast sketch for a decision I then execute with tools that give me pixel control and clean licensing.

Third, and this one surprised me when I started doing it: concept art for things that don't exist yet. A client building a product for a market that has no existing visual language — think early-stage climate tech, or a genuinely novel hardware product — doesn't have competitors I can screenshot for reference. When I was working with a team building sensor hardware for urban farming, there was no "what does this category look like" shortcut. I generated dozens of speculative product shots and environmental scenes — devices embedded in greenhouse structures, abstracted sensor networks, the vibe of "quiet infrastructure" — not because any single image was usable, but because it gave the founders something to react to. Founders are much better at saying "no, not that, colder, more industrial" than they are at generating the first idea from nothing.

Fourth, and more mundane: internal presentation decks. When I'm pitching a creative direction to a client's internal stakeholders — a board, a marketing team, whoever wasn't in the room for the discovery conversations — having a polished, cohesive visual narrative matters more than any individual image being "real." A deck that says "here's the emotional territory we're claiming" benefits from looking considered, even if every image in it is a placeholder generated in an afternoon. This is low-stakes use, and it's the one I feel zero complicated feelings about.

Where It Falls Apart, Specifically

Now the part the AI-optimism posts tend to skip. There are specific, recurring failure modes, and knowing them has saved me from some embarrassing client calls.

Interface generation is the obvious one, but it's worth being precise about why it fails, because the failure mode is instructive. Midjourney has no concept of a design system. It doesn't know what a button is in any functional sense — it knows what buttons tend to look like in the training data, which means it can produce a rounded rectangle with text-shaped marks on it that reads as "button" from six feet away and falls apart the moment you look at alignment, padding consistency, or whether the text is real. I've had clients send me Midjourney-generated "dashboard concepts" they found inspiring and ask if we can "build toward this." The honest answer is always: we can build toward the feeling, not the image. The actual layout decisions — information hierarchy, component states, responsive behavior, accessibility contrast ratios — have to be made from scratch, by a person who understands the product. No amount of prompting fixes this, because the tool isn't reasoning about function, it's pattern-matching on surface appearance.

Text rendering is better than it used to be but still unreliable enough that I never use it for anything with real copy. Logos, wordmarks, labeled UI elements — all unusable. This matters because clients who aren't deep in the tool don't always register that the "Settings" label on a generated screen is actually meaningless squiggles until you zoom in. I've learned to flag this proactively rather than wait for them to notice and feel like something was hidden from them.

Consistency across a set is harder than it looks from the outside. If I need six images that feel like they belong to the same brand universe — same color discipline, same level of abstraction, same "hand" — Midjourney will drift. Lighting shifts, stylistic choices wobble, and what looked like a cohesive set in isolation falls apart when placed side by side on a page. There are techniques to fight this — consistent seed values, reference images, tightly controlled prompt structures — but it's real work, not a toggle you flip. I budget time for this specifically; I don't assume the fifth image will match the first.

Then there's the ownership and licensing question, which I take more seriously than most of the "just use AI" advice I read implies I should. Midjourney's terms have changed over time, and depending on the client's revenue and the specific plan, commercial usage rights differ. I don't treat "the tool's terms of service technically allow it" as the end of the conversation. Some clients — anyone in a regulated industry, anyone with legal counsel paying attention, anyone building a brand they intend to defend aggressively — have explicitly told me they don't want any AI-generated material in deliverables, full stop, because the legal landscape around training data and copyright is still unsettled and they don't want to be a test case. I respect that completely and build it into scoping. I'd rather have that conversation on day one than have a client's lawyer ask about it after launch.

The subtler failure is aesthetic homogeneity. Midjourney has a "look" — a certain hyper-polished, dramatically lit, slightly over-rendered quality — and if you're not careful you end up pushing every client toward that same house style regardless of what actually suits their brand. I've caught myself doing this. Three projects in a row where the mood exploration all trended toward moody cinematic lighting and shallow depth of field because that's what the model defaults to when you don't fight it. That's a real risk for differentiation, which is supposed to be the entire point of hiring a designer instead of using a template. I now treat "does this look like it could belong to any other brand that used the same tool" as a specific checkpoint before I show a client anything.

My Actual Workflow, Step by Step

It's worth walking through what this looks like in practice, because the abstract version ("use it for mood exploration") undersells how much deliberate structure is involved.

Discovery call happens first, always without any AI tool open. I want the client's own words, their own reference points, their own hesitations, unfiltered by whatever imagery I might put in front of them too early. If I open Midjourney before I understand the business, I'm steering the conversation with a tool's aesthetic biases instead of listening.

After that call, I write down the adjectives and tensions that came up — the client's actual words, not my interpretation of them. "Trustworthy but not boring." "Energetic without being a startup cliché." "We want to look expensive but our users are skeptical of anything that looks expensive." These tensions are gold, because they tell me what to avoid as much as what to pursue.

Then I build prompt sets, usually three to five distinct directions, each one deliberately exaggerated in a different way. I'm not trying to find the final answer yet; I'm trying to triangulate. One direction pushed warmer and more organic, one pushed colder and more technical, one somewhere in between. For each direction I generate maybe eight to twelve images, focusing less on any single "hero" image and more on whether the set holds together as a coherent sensibility.

I curate hard before showing anything. This is the step I think a lot of designers skip, and it's the one that actually requires taste rather than prompting skill. Out of forty generated images I might show six, and I'm choosing based on what best represents the direction's extreme, not what's individually prettiest. A client needs to see the edges of a direction to have an informed reaction to it. If I show only the safest, most average images from each set, I get lukewarm "sure, I guess" reactions instead of useful "oh, that's too cold, pull it back" feedback.

The presentation itself matters a lot. I never send raw image dumps. I build a short, structured deck — three or four slides per direction, each framed with the adjectives it's answering, explicitly labeled as "exploratory, not final," with a note on what element of it is working regardless of whether the client likes the image as a whole. ("The direction overall feels too aggressive, but the color pairing in image 3 is exactly the warmth we talked about — I want to carry that forward.") This framing does a lot of work in managing expectations, because it tells the client up front that we're mining these images for decisions, not shipping them.

Once we converge on a direction — and this usually takes one round, occasionally two — I translate the chosen sensibility into an actual design system. Color palette extracted and refined in Figma, not sampled directly from the AI image (because AI-generated color relationships are often subtly impossible to reproduce consistently — slightly inconsistent hex drift between what looks like the same color in different images). Typography chosen to match the tone, not generated. Real photography or illustration commissioned or licensed if the direction calls for human subjects or anything brand-critical, because that's exactly the territory where AI artifacts are most likely to be noticed and most damaging if they are. Midjourney gets me to the decision quickly; it almost never survives into the final file.

There's one more step that I've added only in the last year: a short internal QA pass specifically for "does any part of this read as obviously AI-generated in a way that would embarrass the client." Weird hands, nonsensical text, that uncanny over-smoothness in skin and fabric — I check for these deliberately before anything goes in a deck, because clients have gotten sharper at spotting them, and a client noticing an AI artifact you missed does real damage to their trust in your attention to detail, regardless of how incidental that artifact was to the actual deliverable.

How I Talk About This With Clients

I used to not mention it at all, on the theory that process is my business and the deliverable is theirs. I've changed my mind on this, for a few reasons, and I think the change has made client relationships better, not worse.

First, clients increasingly ask directly. "Did you use AI for this?" is a normal question now, not an accusatory one, and dodging it or being vague reads as evasive even when the honest answer is completely reasonable. I'd rather volunteer it.

Second, being upfront lets me frame it correctly the first time, instead of correcting a misunderstanding later. If a client doesn't know Midjourney was involved in moodboarding, and they later find out, their mental model shifts from "my designer did careful creative exploration" to "my designer used a shortcut," even though the actual work — the curation, the taste, the translation into a real system — didn't change at all. The perception gap is the risk, not the practice itself. So now, in my project kickoff document, there's a short plain paragraph: "Early creative exploration may include AI-generated reference imagery to speed up mood and direction conversations. No AI-generated imagery will appear in final deliverables unless separately discussed and approved." That one sentence has pre-empted every awkward conversation I used to have reactively.

Third, pricing. I don't discount my rate because a tool sped up one phase of the work. This surprises some clients, so I address it directly: the time I save in generating reference images gets reinvested in more rounds of direction exploration and more careful curation, not passed through as a lower invoice. What they're paying for was never "hours spent dragging a mouse" — it's judgment, curation, and the translation of a feeling into a working system, none of which got faster. If anything, the curation step got more important, because now there's more raw material to sift through and more responsibility to pick well. I say this plainly when it comes up, usually during scoping, because I'd rather have that conversation before the invoice than after.

A specific case that sharpened my thinking here: a branding client for a children's education app wanted to use several Midjourney images directly in their pitch deck for investors — not refined, not rebuilt, just the raw output, because they liked them and the deadline was tight. I pushed back, not out of purism, but practically: investor decks get scrutinized, screenshotted, and shared, and a children's product with uncanny AI-generated kid faces in the hero imagery is a real reputational risk, independent of any licensing question. We compromised — illustrated characters in a simplified, clearly stylized direction instead, inspired by the mood we'd found in exploration but built by an illustrator I brought in. The client was annoyed about the extra cost in the moment and thanked me for it later when a competitor got publicly mocked for an obviously AI-generated mascot. That's the kind of judgment call that's actually the job — knowing when the shortcut that worked for internal alignment is wrong for the audience-facing version.

Mistakes I've Made Along the Way

I'll be specific about a few, because "here's what I learned" is more useful than "here's my polished process," and the polished process above only exists because of these.

Early on, I let a client fall in love with a Midjourney mood image before I'd thought through whether it was actually executable. A hospitality brand loved a generated image of a sun-drenched, textured lobby scene with a very specific light quality — warm, grainy, almost film-like — and wanted the entire site's photography to match it exactly. The problem was that light quality came from the model essentially blending a hundred different photography styles into something that doesn't correspond to any single achievable lighting setup or lens. When we hired a photographer to try to recreate it, we spent real budget chasing a reference that was, in a literal sense, not a real photographic look. I learned to sanity-check "is this actually achievable with real photography or real design tools" before presenting a direction as if it were a target, not just an inspiration.

Second mistake: over-relying on a single tool's aesthetic defaults without fighting them, which I mentioned earlier but want to be concrete about. Early in my usage I was taking the first batch of "good enough" images instead of pushing the prompt further, and in hindsight three separate clients from that period have visual identities that are more similar to each other than I'd like, specifically in lighting mood and color grading. That's a direct cost of convenience. Now I deliberately generate against the model's defaults — if the natural output trends toward cool, cinematic, high-contrast, I push prompts toward flat, bright, graphic alternatives specifically to make sure I'm choosing a direction rather than inheriting one.

Third: I once under-communicated the "exploratory only" framing to a client's internal team, and a generated image made its way into an early version of a sales one-pager that got sent to a prospective partner before I'd signed off on final assets. Nobody was trying to deceive anyone — it was a scramble, deadlines were tight, someone grabbed the prettiest image from a shared folder. But it taught me to never put unlabeled exploratory images in any shared folder a client's broader team has access to. Now, anything AI-generated lives in a clearly named "exploration — not for external use" folder, and I say that distinction out loud, more than once, because good intentions don't survive a deadline crunch.

Fourth, and this one is more about my own habits than client-facing risk: I underestimated how much time good curation takes and used to budget mood exploration as a quick, cheap phase. It isn't quick if you're doing it well. Generating images is fast. Looking at sixty images with enough critical distance to pick the six that actually represent distinct, defensible directions — rather than six that are just individually appealing — takes real time and is, frankly, some of the hardest thinking in the whole project, because it's the moment where "what does this brand feel like" gets decided. I now budget it properly instead of treating it as a free byproduct of having a fast tool.

The Honest Verdict

If you're a designer wondering whether to bring Midjourney into client work, here's the version of my answer that isn't trying to sell you anything. It is genuinely useful, but for a narrower slice of the process than the hype suggests, and the value is almost entirely in compressing the fuzzy, subjective, pre-verbal part of a project where client and designer are trying to agree on a feeling before either of them can describe it in words. It is not a shortcut to finished interface design, finished brand assets, or anything that needs to survive close inspection, real text, or legal scrutiny. The gap between "impressive first look" and "actually usable" is still enormous, and pretending otherwise to a client is the fastest way to damage the trust that your actual expertise depends on.

The skill that makes it worth using isn't prompting — prompting is a few afternoons of practice, genuinely not more than that. The skill is knowing which fifteen percent of a project benefits from this kind of fast, disposable visual exploration, and then being disciplined enough to treat the output as disposable: a scaffold for a decision, not the decision itself. Clients aren't paying me for access to a tool they could open themselves for twenty dollars a month. They're paying for the judgment that decides what to do with what the tool produces, the willingness to throw away the forty images that didn't work, and the craft to rebuild the one image that did into something that will actually hold up as a real brand, a real interface, a real product people trust. That part hasn't gotten any faster, and honestly, I hope it never does.
