Webflow or WordPress: 5 Real Scenarios From My Practice

Sep 21, 2026 · 17 min read

Why this question keeps coming up in every kickoff call

Almost every discovery call I run eventually lands on the same question: "So, Webflow or WordPress?" Clients ask it like they're asking me to pick a winner in a boxing match. I get it — they've read a dozen comparison articles, watched some YouTube videos, and now they want a clean answer before they sign a contract. But after eight years of building sites on both platforms, I can tell you the honest answer is almost never "Webflow is better" or "WordPress is better." It's "better for what, exactly, and who's going to touch this thing after I leave?"

The platform decision isn't really a design decision. It's an operations decision disguised as a design decision. Who updates the content? How fast does the team move? What's the budget for hosting and plugins versus a monthly Webflow seat? Is there already a marketing team with an established workflow, or is this a solo founder who will be editing their own homepage copy at 11pm before a fundraising call?

I've stopped answering this question in the abstract. Instead, I walk through what actually happened on real projects — the good outcomes and the ones where I picked wrong and had to eat some hours fixing it. Below are five scenarios pulled directly from my client work over the past few years. Names and identifying details are changed, but the situations, the constraints, and the mistakes are real. If you're currently staring at this same decision, one of these five will probably look familiar.

Scenario 1: The agency that needed a launch-ready site in 12 days

A boutique branding agency hired me to redesign their own marketing site. They had just repositioned themselves after losing a big retainer client, and they wanted the new site live before a conference where three of their partners would be speaking. Twelve working days from kickoff to launch. No content management complexity — five pages, a case studies grid, a blog they'd update maybe once a month, and a contact form.

This is the textbook Webflow scenario, and I want to explain exactly why, because the reasons matter more than the conclusion.

First, the design-to-build handoff disappears entirely. In a WordPress project, even a simple one, there's a translation step: I design in Figma, then either I or a developer rebuilds that design as a theme, templates, and custom fields. Every animation, every hover state, every weird grid layout has to be re-implemented in PHP and CSS, and something always gets lost or simplified in that translation — a transition timing that felt right in the prototype ends up "close enough" in the build because nobody has time to fuss over 200ms of ease-in-out on a Tuesday before launch.

With Webflow, I designed and built simultaneously. The hover states, the scroll-triggered reveals on the case studies grid, the custom cursor on the hero — I built those directly in the Webflow Designer, tested them in the browser in real time, and there was no handoff at all. What I saw in the canvas was what shipped. On a 12-day timeline, cutting out an entire round of developer implementation and QA against a static design file is the difference between hitting the deadline and missing it.

Second, hosting and infrastructure were a non-issue. I didn't have to think about a staging server, SSL certificates, database backups, or which managed WordPress host would give us decent uptime for the conference traffic spike. I published, connected their existing domain, and moved on to actual design work. For a 12-day sprint, every hour not spent on DevOps is an hour spent on the thing the client is actually paying for.

Third — and this is the part people underestimate — the agency's own team was going to update this site themselves going forward, and none of them were developers. They were designers and strategists who understood visual hierarchy and could read a Figma file, but had zero interest in learning a WordPress dashboard, page builders, or how custom fields work. Webflow's Editor mode let me hand off a site where a non-technical person could swap out a case study image, edit a headline, or add a new team member card without breaking the layout. I did a 45-minute training call, and that was the entire onboarding.

The site launched on day 11. The one thing I'll flag honestly: Webflow's native blog CMS is fine for five posts a month, but it starts to feel thin once you need serious editorial workflows — more on that in scenario two. For this agency, that limitation never mattered. They wanted speed, visual precision, and a client-editable site with zero server maintenance. Webflow gave them all three without compromise.

Scenario 2: The content publisher with three editors and a daily posting schedule

This one is the mirror image, and it's the project that permanently cured me of any "Webflow for everything" instinct I might have had.

The client ran a niche B2B media site — daily articles, a weekly newsletter roundup pulled from tagged content, guest contributor accounts, and an editorial calendar with three people publishing independently, often multiple times a day. They came to me wanting a redesign, and their instinct going in was Webflow, because they'd seen how clean Webflow sites looked and their previous WordPress site felt cluttered and slow. The problem wasn't WordPress. The problem was five years of plugin accumulation and a theme nobody understood anymore.

I ran a short audit before committing to a platform, and a few things became clear immediately. They needed role-based permissions — editors who could publish, contributors who could only submit for review, and an admin who approved everything before it went live. They needed a genuinely fast content-entry workflow because their editors were writing 800-1200 word articles daily and needed rich formatting, embedded tweets, inline images, and pull quotes without fighting the CMS. They needed categories, tags, and a taxonomy that could support a "related articles" system and an internal search that actually understood their content structure. And they needed this to keep working reliably as their catalog grew past a few thousand articles.

Webflow's CMS is genuinely good for structured content — case studies, team bios, product listings, portfolio items. But it was never built to be a newsroom. There's no native contributor-review workflow with the granularity this team needed. Rich text editing inside Webflow's CMS fields is serviceable but noticeably more limited than the WordPress block editor once you're writing long-form content daily. And at their volume, CMS item limits and collection restrictions on Webflow plans would have forced them into constant tier upgrades or workaround architecture — splitting content across multiple collections in ways that made the "related content" logic a nightmare to maintain.

So I recommended WordPress, but not the tangled version they'd inherited. I rebuilt it on a clean, modern block theme, stripped their plugin list from around 40 down to eleven that actually earned their place, and configured their editorial roles properly using WordPress's native user role system extended with a lightweight editorial workflow plugin. I designed the actual visual system in Figma first — this is where the "WordPress looks worse" perception usually comes from, honestly. Most dated-looking WordPress sites aren't dated because of WordPress; they're dated because nobody did real design work before building the theme. I built a custom block theme with proper typography, spacing, and a component library in the Gutenberg editor so their writers had branded, on-design blocks — pull quotes, author bylines, newsletter CTAs — available directly in their writing interface without needing a developer every time they wanted to publish a slightly different layout.

The end result loaded faster and looked cleaner than their old site, ran comfortably on a mid-tier managed WordPress host, and gave their editorial team exactly the publishing workflow they needed. Their monthly hosting cost less than a single Webflow CMS plan seat would have, and it scaled past ten thousand articles without me touching the architecture again. This is the scenario where WordPress isn't the compromise choice — it's the correct engineering choice, and Webflow would have been the wrong tool no matter how nice the marketing pages could have looked.

Scenario 3: The bootstrapped e-commerce founder with a $40/month budget

A solo founder came to me wanting to launch a small direct-to-consumer skincare line — maybe 15 products at launch, planning to grow the catalog slowly, running on savings with no outside funding. Budget was the entire conversation. She'd already burned money on a Shopify trial and felt like the transaction fees and app costs were going to eat her margins before she sold a single bottle.

This is a scenario where I actually pushed back on both Webflow and the platform she assumed she needed, and it's worth walking through the math because it applies to a lot of early-stage founders.

Webflow's e-commerce plans are clean and the checkout experience is genuinely nice, but the pricing structure includes both a monthly platform fee and a transaction fee on top, and the more advanced inventory and product variant handling she needed — different sizes, bundles, subscription options down the line — pushed her into a higher tier than the entry e-commerce plan. When I ran the actual numbers against her projected order volume, Webflow e-commerce was going to cost noticeably more per month than she wanted to commit to before she had proven demand.

I built her site on WordPress with WooCommerce instead, on a modest but reliable host, using a lean, custom-built theme rather than a bloated pre-made WooCommerce theme (this matters — most of the "WordPress e-commerce is slow" complaints I see trace back to a theme with forty unused features running on shared hosting, not to WooCommerce itself). WooCommerce has no transaction fee layered on top of your payment processor's normal rate, which for a low-margin consumer product matters enormously. Product variants, simple subscription add-ons, and discount codes were all handled through free or genuinely low-cost plugins rather than being locked behind a platform tier upgrade.

Her all-in monthly cost — hosting, domain, SSL, a couple of essential plugins — came in under $40 a month, versus a real projected cost more than double that on Webflow's e-commerce tier once transaction volume picked up. For a founder testing product-market fit with thin margins, that gap is not trivial. It's the difference between reinvesting in inventory and bleeding cash on software before she's sold enough to know if the business works.

I want to be fair to Webflow here, because this isn't a universal rule — for a higher-margin product, a founder who values design polish over per-unit cost, or someone selling a handful of high-ticket items rather than a growing SKU catalog, Webflow e-commerce can be the better call, especially paired with a service like a fulfillment partner that plugs into it cleanly. The lesson from this project isn't "WordPress beats Webflow for e-commerce." It's that e-commerce is the one category where you should build an actual cost model against your specific margins and volume before choosing a platform, because the pricing structures diverge in ways that compound fast at scale.

Scenario 4: The client who swore they'd "manage it themselves" after handoff

This scenario burned me once, and I've built it into every discovery call since. A mid-sized consulting firm hired me for a full site redesign. During kickoff, the founder was adamant: "We want something we can manage ourselves — no developer on retainer, no monthly fees to an agency, our office manager will handle updates." Based on that brief, and because the site itself was fairly simple — services pages, a team directory, a resources section — I built it in Webflow. Clean CMS collections for the team and resources, Editor-mode access for the office manager, everything documented.

Three months after launch, I got a call. The office manager who was supposed to maintain the site had left the company. Her replacement had never used Webflow, found the Designer view intimidating even though I'd locked her into Editor mode, and more importantly, the firm's IT-adjacent handyman — the guy who "handled computer stuff" for them informally — didn't know Webflow at all. He knew WordPress. Every small business's ecosystem has one of these people: not a professional developer, but the person who gets called when something breaks, and that person's existing skill set quietly determines what platform actually gets maintained long-term.

Nothing was broken. The site worked fine. But "manage it ourselves" turned out to mean something different in practice than it meant in the kickoff call. What they actually meant was "manage it within the tools and people we already have relationships with," and Webflow, despite being objectively easier to learn for a single trained user, was a foreign system to everyone in their existing support network except the one person who'd since left.

I ended up doing a paid engagement six months later helping them either train a new person properly or consider a WordPress rebuild — we went with proper retraining rather than a rebuild, since the site itself was working well, but it cost them money and stress that a five-minute question in the original kickoff would have avoided.

The lesson I took from this, and now ask explicitly in every first call: "Who specifically is going to log in and make changes six months from now, and what do they already know?" Not "are you comfortable with technology" — that's a useless question, everyone says yes. I ask about their actual existing support relationships. Do they have an IT person, a VA, a marketing coordinator who's used WordPress at a previous job? Is there already institutional knowledge sitting somewhere in the organization that should tip the platform choice? A platform is only as maintainable as the humans available to maintain it, and that pool of humans is dramatically larger and more fungible for WordPress simply because of its market share — there are more WordPress-literate marketing coordinators, VAs, and freelance help available at short notice than Webflow-literate ones, even though Webflow itself is arguably easier to pick up from zero.

This doesn't mean WordPress wins every "will this outlive my involvement" conversation. It means the honest answer requires asking about the client's actual human infrastructure, not just the platform's feature set, and I now build that question into every proposal before I recommend anything.

Scenario 5: The SaaS marketing team running constant experiments

A Series A SaaS company brought me in to redesign their marketing site — homepage, pricing, a handful of landing pages for paid campaigns, and a "solutions by industry" section they wanted to expand over time. Their marketing team was three people: a head of growth who ran paid acquisition, a content marketer, and a designer who reported to me during the engagement but would own ongoing design after I left.

The defining constraint here wasn't budget or content volume — it was velocity and experimentation. The head of growth wanted to spin up new landing page variants weekly for different ad campaigns, test headline and hero variations, and kill underperforming pages within days. This is a fundamentally different rhythm from either the agency site in scenario one or the publisher in scenario two.

I chose Webflow, and the deciding factor was less about the CMS and more about how fast a non-developer could duplicate an existing landing page, swap out a hero section, adjust the layout slightly for a specific campaign, and publish — all without touching code or waiting on a dev sprint. In WordPress, even with a modern block editor and a solid theme, spinning up true design variants at that pace usually means either a page builder plugin bolted onto the CMS (which reintroduces a lot of the bloat and inconsistency problems from scenario two) or actual developer time for anything beyond swapping text and images.

I built a component-based system in Webflow using nested symbols and reusable sections — hero variants, pricing table components, testimonial blocks — so the growth lead could assemble new landing pages almost like arranging Lego pieces within the constraints I'd designed, rather than starting from a blank canvas or needing a developer for each variant. Webflow's built-in staging and version history also mattered here in a very practical way: when a campaign page underperformed and they wanted to revert to a previous version quickly, that was a few clicks, not a restore-from-backup request to a hosting provider.

I'll also mention what didn't work as cleanly: their engineering team eventually wanted to pull structured pricing data from the marketing site into their own product dashboard via API, and Webflow's CMS API, while functional, required more custom glue code and ongoing maintenance than a headless WordPress setup with a well-established REST or GraphQL layer would have. This wasn't enough to change the platform decision — the marketing velocity need dominated everything else — but it's the kind of integration cost that's easy to overlook in the initial platform conversation and worth surfacing early if a marketing site needs to talk to internal tooling down the line.

Nine months after launch, the growth team had shipped over 60 landing page variants without a single developer request. That's the scenario Webflow was genuinely built for — not because it's a "better" platform in the abstract, but because rapid, designer-owned iteration was the actual job the site needed to do, and the tooling matched the job.

The framework I actually use now, in order

I don't run a formal scoring matrix with every client, but after these five projects and dozens of others like them, I ask myself the same sequence of questions before recommending a platform, and I'd suggest anyone facing this decision do the same rather than starting from brand preference or what a competitor used.

  • Who touches this after launch, and what do they already know? This is the question I skipped in scenario four and now ask first, every time. Not "how technical is your team" in the abstract, but specifically which named person or role will log in monthly, and what platform experience already exists in your organization's support network — internal staff, existing contractors, or agency relationships.
  • What's the actual content volume and workflow complexity? A handful of pages updated occasionally is a completely different problem than daily editorial output with multiple contributors and approval chains. If you need role-based publishing workflows, high-volume content, or complex taxonomy, that alone points strongly toward WordPress regardless of other factors.
  • How much does design iteration speed matter, and who owns it? If a designer or marketer needs to ship new page variants weekly without developer involvement, and the content structure is relatively simple, Webflow's direct-manipulation model usually wins. If design changes are infrequent and content changes are constant, that balance flips.
  • What does the real cost model look like at your actual volume, not launch-day volume? This applies most sharply to e-commerce, but it's true everywhere — model your costs at six months and eighteen months of realistic usage, not the pricing page's cheapest tier. Webflow's per-seat and CMS-tier pricing and WordPress's hosting-plus-plugin costs scale very differently depending on traffic, content volume, and team size.
  • Does this site need to talk to other systems? If there's a real integration need — a product dashboard pulling pricing data, a CRM syncing form submissions in complex ways, custom authentication, a membership system with intricate permission tiers — audit what that actually requires on each platform before committing. Sometimes the answer still favors Webflow with a well-planned API layer; sometimes it tips firmly toward WordPress's more mature plugin and integration ecosystem.

None of these five questions has a universal winner baked in, which is exactly why I stopped giving clients a platform opinion in the first call. I give them these questions instead, and the platform tends to answer itself by the second conversation.

What I'd push back on hardest is choosing a platform based on how a portfolio site looks in a screenshot, or based on which platform a competitor happens to use, or based on which one you personally find more fun to build in. Those are real considerations, but they're the last things I weigh, not the first. The agency in scenario one and the publisher in scenario two ended up on opposite platforms and both got exactly the right tool for their actual operating reality. That's the outcome worth optimizing for — not winning an abstract argument about which platform is "better," but matching the tool to the specific humans, workflows, and growth trajectory sitting in front of you.
