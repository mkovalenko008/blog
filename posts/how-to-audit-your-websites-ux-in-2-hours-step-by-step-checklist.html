How to Audit Your Website's UX in 2 Hours: A Checklist

Sep 1, 2026 · 17 min read

Why two hours is enough to find the real problems

Every client I've worked with has, at some point, asked for "a full UX audit." What they usually mean is a two-week deliverable with heatmaps, screen recordings, a competitive analysis deck, and a 40-page PDF nobody on the team will read past page six. That kind of audit has its place, but it's not what most websites need first. Most websites need someone to sit down, use the thing like a real visitor would, and write down every point of friction before they lose the thread of what's actually wrong.

I've run this two-hour version dozens of times — for my own clients before a redesign kickoff, for agencies who inherited a site mid-project, and for founders who just want a gut check before spending money on a rebuild. It consistently surfaces 80% of the issues a full audit would find, because most UX problems aren't hidden. They're sitting right on the homepage, in the checkout flow, in the contact form that silently fails on mobile Safari. You don't need six weeks of research to notice a submit button that's the same color as the background.

The point of a timeboxed audit isn't to be lazy about it. It's to force prioritization. When you have two hours, you stop chasing every micro-detail and start hunting for the handful of issues that are actually costing conversions, trust, or time. That's a more useful output for a client than a document that treats a slightly-too-small line-height with the same urgency as a broken checkout.

This checklist is built to be run in one sitting, in order, with a timer. It assumes you have access to the live site, analytics if possible (not required), and a notebook or a simple doc where you're logging findings as you go — not at the end. Let's get into it.

Before you start: setup that takes 10 minutes

Skipping setup is the single biggest reason self-run audits turn into unfocused browsing sessions. Spend the first ten minutes doing this and the rest of the audit runs itself.

  • Define who you're pretending to be. Pick one primary user persona and one primary goal — "a new visitor from a Google search trying to understand if this agency does branding work" or "a returning customer trying to reorder." Write it at the top of your notes. Every judgment call during the audit gets filtered through this lens, not your personal taste.
  • Clear your cache or open an incognito window. You need to see what a first-time visitor sees, not a version pre-loaded with your saved logins and dismissed cookie banners.
  • Open three environments. Desktop Chrome, a real mobile device (not just DevTools' responsive mode — actual touch targets and actual load times matter), and, if you have it, a slow-connection throttle. Devices lie to each other; a layout that's fine on your MacBook is routinely broken on a mid-range Android.
  • Pull up analytics for five minutes, if available. You don't need a deep dive — just the top 5 landing pages by traffic, the bounce rate on those pages, and the drop-off point in any funnel (cart, form, signup). This tells you where to spend your limited time. If there's no analytics access, default to homepage, main product/service page, and the primary conversion form.
  • Set a timer for each section below. This is the part people skip and regret. Without a timer you'll spend 40 minutes on the homepage and 5 on checkout, which is backwards for most sites.

One more thing worth saying: write down what's wrong before you write down why. It's tempting to diagnose and prescribe in the same breath, but that slows you down and often leads to solutioning too early. Log the symptom, move on, come back to fixes in the last 20 minutes.

Minutes 10–30: The first 8 seconds, stress-tested

This block is about the homepage — or, if most traffic lands elsewhere, whatever page most people actually see first. Load the page cold, start a stopwatch, and give yourself exactly 8 seconds before you're allowed to scroll. Then answer this out loud or in writing: what does this company do, and who is it for?

If you can't answer confidently, that's not a nitpick — it's the finding. I've audited sites for genuinely good businesses where the homepage headline was a vague statement like "Building the future of connection" with no indication of industry, audience, or offer. Clever taglines are a luxury only very well-known brands can afford. Everyone else needs clarity before cleverness.

Run through this list on the hero section and the visible-without-scrolling area:

  • Value proposition test. Does the headline state what the business does, for whom, and why it matters — in plain language a stranger would understand? If it takes rereading twice, flag it.
  • Primary call-to-action. Is there exactly one obvious next step, or are there four competing buttons ("Get Started," "Learn More," "Book a Demo," "Contact Us") with no visual hierarchy telling the visitor which one matters most?
  • Visual hierarchy check. Squint at the page, or blur your eyes slightly. What's the first thing your eye lands on? Is it the thing that should be first — usually the value proposition or the CTA — or is it a decorative image, a slider animation, or a logo carousel?
  • Trust signals above the fold. For anything transactional (agencies, SaaS, ecommerce), is there any indication of credibility visible early — client logos, a review count, a recognizable brand mention? Not mandatory, but its absence matters more on cold-traffic landing pages.
  • Load time, felt not measured. You don't need Lighthouse open for this part (that comes later) — just notice: did the hero content appear instantly, or did you sit looking at a blank screen or a layout jump while fonts and images popped in?

Then scroll through the rest of the homepage at a normal pace — the pace an impatient visitor would use, which is faster than you think — and log anything that breaks the scanning rhythm: walls of text with no subheadings, sections whose purpose isn't clear from a glance, stock photography that says nothing about the actual business, or CTAs repeated so identically that none of them feel like "the" action.

A useful trick here: read only the headings and subheadings on the page, skipping all body copy. If the page still tells a coherent story using just those, the content hierarchy is doing its job. If it reads like a random list of section titles, the structure needs work regardless of how good the copy underneath is.

Minutes 30–60: Navigation and information architecture

This is where most audits either find gold or find nothing, depending on how deliberately you test it. Random clicking around a nav menu doesn't reveal IA problems — task-based navigation does.

Write down three to five realistic tasks a visitor would come to the site to do. For an agency site: "find pricing," "find a case study in a specific industry," "find out how to contact the team." For ecommerce: "find the return policy," "find a specific product category," "check if a product ships internationally." For SaaS: "find out if there's a free trial," "find integration details," "find how to cancel."

For each task, time yourself completing it and count the clicks. Then evaluate:

  • Did you find it in under three clicks? Past three clicks, most real users would have given up or opened a support chat instead. If a task takes four or five clicks, that's a structural flag, not a UI polish issue.
  • Did the nav labels match your mental model? This is the single most common IA failure I see. Companies love internal jargon in their navigation — "Solutions," "Platform," "Ecosystem" — that means something to the team and nothing to a first-time visitor. If you hesitated over which nav item to click for a task, so will everyone else.
  • Is there a search function, and does it work? Type a real product name or a real question into site search if one exists. A surprising number of site searches return zero results for terms that clearly exist on the site — usually because search indexes only page titles, not content.
  • Breadcrumbs and "where am I" cues. On any site deeper than three levels, check whether a visitor can tell where they are in the structure and how to get back up a level without hitting the browser back button repeatedly.
  • Footer audit. Footers are where dying information architecture goes to hide. Check whether the footer duplicates the header nav uselessly, or whether it actually surfaces the secondary things people look for — legal pages, sitemap, secondary contact methods, social proof.

While you're moving through the nav, keep a running note of dead ends: pages with no clear next step, pages that don't link back to anything relevant, orphaned content that seems to exist only because someone once needed it. These accumulate on sites that have been live for a few years without IA maintenance, and they quietly increase bounce rate on pages that otherwise have decent content.

One more check that takes thirty seconds and catches real problems: open the mobile nav (the hamburger menu) and count how many taps it takes to close it, and whether it traps focus correctly. Menus that don't close on link click, or that require scrolling within an already-scrolled page, are common enough to check every time.

Minutes 60–90: Walk the core conversion path end to end

This is the highest-value 30 minutes in the entire audit, and it's the one most people rush because it requires actually completing a form, a checkout, or a signup — sometimes with fake data, sometimes for real. Do it anyway. Reading a form is not the same as filling it out.

Identify the one flow that matters most to the business — checkout for ecommerce, a contact/quote form for an agency or B2B service, signup for SaaS, a booking flow for a service business — and go through it as your defined persona would, start to finish, on both desktop and mobile.

Things to check at each step:

  • Field count and necessity. Count every input field. For each one, ask whether it's truly needed at this stage or could be collected later. A contact form asking for company size, budget range, and how-did-you-hear-about-us before someone has even gotten a reply is friction that costs completions, especially on mobile.
  • Label and placeholder clarity. Click into each field. If the label disappears the moment you start typing (placeholder-as-label, a pattern that should have died years ago), check whether you can still tell what the field is for once it's populated. This trips people up constantly on longer forms when they scroll back to check an entry.
  • Error handling, on purpose. Deliberately submit the form wrong — leave a required field blank, enter an invalid email, use a phone number in the wrong format. Does the error message appear near the field it refers to, in plain language, without the whole form resetting or scrolling to the top and losing your other entries? Broken error handling is one of the most common silent killers of conversion rate, because it doesn't show up unless you actually trigger it.
  • Progress indication on multi-step flows. If it's more than one screen, is there a visible sense of how many steps remain? Unlabeled multi-step flows create abandonment because users can't judge the remaining effort.
  • Confirmation and next steps. After successful submission, is it obvious it worked? Vague or missing confirmation ("thank you" with no indication of what happens next, or worse, a redirect back to the homepage with no message at all) leaves users unsure whether to try again, which sometimes causes duplicate submissions.
  • Payment-specific checks, for ecommerce. Are shipping costs and taxes shown before the final step, or sprung on the user at the last screen? Is guest checkout available, or is account creation forced? Both are well-documented, well-studied causes of cart abandonment, and both are trivially easy to check in five minutes.

Do this same flow on your mobile device, not just resized desktop Chrome. Tap targets that seemed fine on a mouse cursor are routinely too small or too close together for a thumb — the classic case being two buttons or two links close enough that a slightly-off tap hits the wrong one. Also check whether the mobile keyboard that pops up matches the field type: an email field should trigger the @ key layout, a phone field should trigger the numeric keypad. If it doesn't, that's a small but real friction point that adds up across a form with several fields.

If the flow involves a login or account area, create a throwaway test account and go through onboarding as a brand-new user would. First-run experiences are notoriously under-tested internally because everyone on the team already has an account with data in it — they never see the empty state, the confusing first-login screen, or the onboarding tour that references features not yet visible.

Minutes 90–110: Mobile, speed, and accessibility spot-checks

You've already been testing on mobile throughout the previous sections, which is intentional — mobile shouldn't be a separate afterthought pass, it should be baked into every task you've run so far. This block is for the checks that are specifically about technical performance and accessibility, which are easy to overlook when you're focused on flows and copy.

For speed, run the homepage and one deep page through a free tool — Google PageSpeed Insights or GTmetrix both work and take under two minutes each. You're not trying to become a performance engineer in this audit; you're looking for red flags:

  • Largest Contentful Paint over 2.5 seconds. This usually means an unoptimized hero image or video, and it's often a five-minute fix (compression, correct format, lazy-loading below the fold) with outsized impact on bounce rate.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift. Notice if content visibly jumps as the page loads — images without reserved dimensions, ads or embeds that push content down, fonts that swap and reflow text. This is one of the more annoying-to-users, easy-to-miss-by-teams issues because it happens in the first second of load, which the person who built the page has usually stopped watching by the time they're testing.
  • Mobile-specific score gaps. It's common for desktop performance to be fine and mobile performance to be genuinely bad, because mobile connections and processors expose inefficiencies desktop hides. If there's a large gap, flag it as its own line item — it needs separate attention, not a shared fix.

For accessibility, you don't need a full WCAG audit in two hours, but these checks take five minutes and catch real issues:

  • Keyboard navigation. Put your mouse down. Use only Tab, Shift+Tab, and Enter to navigate the homepage and one form. Can you reach every interactive element in a logical order, and can you see a visible focus indicator at each stop? Sites with no visible focus state are unusable for keyboard-only users, and this is shockingly common even on otherwise well-designed sites, because it never comes up in mouse-based QA.
  • Color contrast on key text. Use a contrast checker browser extension, or eyeball it against WCAG's 4.5:1 ratio for body text — light gray text on white backgrounds is the most common offender, usually introduced because it "looks clean" in a design file at 100% zoom on a calibrated monitor.
  • Image alt text on meaningful images. Right-click or inspect a handful of content-relevant images (not decorative ones) and check whether alt text exists and is descriptive rather than empty or filled with the filename.
  • Zoom test. Zoom the browser to 200% and check whether the layout still works — text doesn't overlap, nothing gets cut off, horizontal scrolling doesn't appear where it shouldn't. This simulates both low-vision users and, practically, anyone on an oddly-sized screen.

None of this requires specialized tools beyond what's free and already in your browser. The goal in this block isn't compliance certification — it's catching the handful of accessibility gaps that also happen to be usability gaps for everyone, which is most of them.

Minutes 110–120: Prioritize findings into something actionable

By this point you'll have a long, messy list — anywhere from fifteen to fifty items depending on the site's age and complexity. The last ten minutes are about turning that list into something a client or team can actually act on, because an unprioritized list of every flaw is not useful; it reads as either overwhelming or nitpicky, and often both.

Sort everything into three buckets, fast, using gut judgment rather than a formal scoring model — you don't have time for a formal model, and honestly for this kind of pass, gut judgment from someone who just spent two hours in the product is a legitimate method:

  • Fix this week. Broken forms, missing error states, anything blocking a core conversion path, anything that's an outright bug rather than a design choice. These are usually cheap to fix and expensive to leave broken.
  • Fix this quarter. Navigation relabeling, homepage hierarchy issues, mobile tap target sizing, performance optimization on key pages. These need some design or dev time but don't require a full project.
  • Consider for the next redesign. Deeper IA restructuring, a full visual refresh, anything that would require rebuilding rather than patching. These are legitimate findings but shouldn't block action on the first two buckets while everyone waits for a bigger project to kick off.

For each item in the first bucket especially, write one sentence connecting the issue to a business impact, not just a UX principle. "The email field doesn't validate format before submission, which likely causes lost leads when users mistype and never notice" lands very differently with a stakeholder than "email field lacks inline validation." The first sentence gets budget approved; the second gets filed away as a nice-to-have.

If you're presenting this to a client rather than acting on it yourself, resist the urge to include everything you found. A report with sixty items feels thorough to write and paralyzing to receive. I've found the sweet spot is five to ten items per bucket, maximum — anything beyond that either wasn't important enough to make the cut, or belongs in a follow-up deeper audit rather than this pass.

It's also worth explicitly naming what you didn't have time to check, rather than letting the client assume the two-hour audit was exhaustive. Things like full accessibility compliance testing, actual user testing with real target users, SEO technical audit, or cross-browser testing beyond Chrome and one mobile device are all reasonable things to flag as "out of scope for this pass" so nobody mistakes a fast diagnostic for a comprehensive one.

Making this a repeatable habit, not a one-off

The real value of a checklist like this isn't the single audit — it's what happens when you run it on the same site every quarter, or run it on every new client project before you touch a single design file. Patterns emerge that a one-time snapshot can't show you: whether the same category of issue (form friction, mobile performance, unclear navigation labels) keeps resurfacing after fixes, which usually points to a process problem rather than a design problem — maybe new pages are being published without anyone checking them against a baseline, or maybe there's no one accountable for reviewing forms after copy changes.

For freelancers and agencies specifically, I'd suggest running this exact sequence as the very first working session on any new engagement, before any strategy calls or mood boards. It gives you concrete, specific things to talk about in the kickoff meeting instead of abstract questions like "what's your brand personality," and it usually earns immediate credibility with a client because you're showing up with observations about their actual product rather than a generic framework. It also protects you later — if you flagged the broken checkout error handling in week one and the client deprioritized it, that's now documented, not something you get blamed for in week twelve.

The two-hour constraint is the feature, not a compromise. It forces you to trust your instincts as a practitioner instead of hiding behind exhaustive process, and it produces a document someone will actually read and act on this month — which, compared to a thorough audit that sits in a shared drive unopened, is the better outcome almost every time.
