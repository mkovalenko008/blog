AI Instead of Stock Photos: When It Works and When It Looks Fake

Sep 27, 2026 · 18 min read

Why this question is suddenly on every project brief

Two years ago, nobody asked me to "just generate the hero image with AI." Now it comes up on almost every project — usually from a client who has already played with Midjourney or ChatGPT's image tools over the weekend and wants to know why they should pay a stock license fee when they can type a sentence and get something "close enough." The honest answer is: sometimes they shouldn't pay for stock. And sometimes AI imagery will quietly sabotage the credibility of the entire site.

The problem isn't the technology. Diffusion models are good enough now that the old tells — six-fingered hands, melted typography, waxy skin — are becoming rarer with every model update. The problem is that "good enough to not look broken" and "good enough to build trust" are two very different bars, and most teams evaluating AI image tools are only checking against the first one.

This article is my attempt to lay out, from actual client work, where AI-generated imagery holds up under scrutiny and where it collapses the moment a real person looks at it for more than three seconds. It's not a takedown of the tools — I use them constantly — and it's not a sales pitch either. It's a set of decision criteria I've built up from watching AI images succeed on some pages and get quietly swapped out on others after user testing.

The economics that started this conversation

Stock photography has always had a credibility problem of its own. Anyone who's spent time in the industry can spot a Shutterstock "diverse team high-fiving in a bright open office" from a thumbnail. Stock photos carry their own uncanny valley — the lighting is too even, the smiles are too symmetrical, the laptop screens are always blank or showing a fake dashboard. Clients have been tolerating this for a decade because the alternative was commissioning real photography, and that's expensive and slow.

AI changed the calculation because it made "custom-looking" imagery available at stock-photo prices, sometimes for free. That's a genuinely disruptive shift for budget-constrained projects — a five-page site for a local business, an internal tool's marketing page, a pitch deck that needs supporting visuals by Thursday. The question was never "is AI as good as a photographer." It was "is AI better than generic stock," and for a specific category of use cases, the answer is clearly yes.

Where AI imagery actually wins

Let me start with the cases where I now default to AI without much hesitation, because these are the situations where stock photography's weaknesses are more damaging than AI's.

Abstract, conceptual, and background imagery

Anything that isn't meant to represent a specific real person, product, or place is fair game. Gradient blobs, textured backgrounds, conceptual illustrations of "data flowing between systems," abstract representations of "growth" or "connection" — this is where AI tools genuinely outperform stock libraries, because stock libraries were never good at this category either. Nobody was getting a great abstract technology background from Shutterstock; they were getting the same overused blue circuit-board photo everyone else licensed too. AI generates infinite, unique variations of this kind of visual filler, and because there's no expectation of realism, there's no uncanny valley to fall into.

I use AI-generated abstract imagery constantly for section dividers, blog post headers, and pattern backgrounds. Nobody has ever flagged one of these as "fake" because there was never a "real" version to compare it to.

Illustrative and stylized work

The moment you move away from photorealism into illustration, flat design, isometric scenes, or painterly styles, AI stops needing to fool anyone. It just needs to look good and be consistent. This is one of the strongest use cases I've found: generating a consistent illustration style across a full site or app when the budget doesn't support a custom illustrator, or when the illustrator's timeline doesn't match the launch date.

The key here is consistency, not individual image quality. A single AI illustration is easy; twelve AI illustrations that look like they belong to the same visual system is the actual skill. That takes a locked style reference, consistent prompt structure, and usually a few rounds of manual color correction to make sure the palette matches the brand rather than whatever the model defaulted to.

Placeholder and early-stage visuals

During wireframing and early prototyping, AI-generated imagery is close to perfect. Clients evaluating layout and hierarchy don't need final photography — they need something that occupies the space with roughly the right tone, so they stop getting distracted by "who is that stock model" and start looking at the actual design decisions. I generate placeholder imagery constantly for Figma files that will later get real photography or approved final assets. It saves real money, because nobody is licensing stock photos for a mockup that might change three times before final sign-off.

Speculative and impossible scenarios

Sometimes a brief calls for something that doesn't exist and couldn't be photographed even with an unlimited budget — a futuristic cityscape for a speculative product concept, a fantasy environment for a game studio's pitch deck, an "imagine if" scenario for an innovation lab. Stock photography simply doesn't have inventory for this. Commissioning a 3D artist or illustrator is the traditional route, and it's still often the better one for final production assets, but for early concept work, AI generation of impossible scenes is faster than anything else available and nobody expects it to be a photograph of something real.

High-volume, low-scrutiny content

Blog posts, internal newsletters, social media filler, email header graphics — content that gets glanced at once and never studied. The scrutiny bar here is genuinely low. Readers are not zooming into the background of a blog header image looking for extra fingers. This is where the volume advantage of AI generation matters most: a content team that needs forty unique header images a month is not going to license forty stock photos or commission forty illustrations, and AI closes that gap cleanly.

Where it falls apart, and why

Now the harder half — the situations where AI imagery reliably damages trust, and the specific reasons why, because "it just looks fake" isn't actionable feedback for a design team trying to make a decision.

Anything claiming to represent a real customer or team member

This is the clearest line, and it's as much an ethical one as a technical one. If a testimonials section implies "these are our actual customers," using AI-generated faces is a trust violation the moment it's discovered — and it will be discovered, because someone will eventually reverse-image-search a testimonial photo, or a competitor will notice the same suspiciously perfect person appearing on three different companies' about pages. I've seen agencies quietly use AI faces for "team member" placeholders that never got swapped before launch, and it is one of the fastest ways to torch credibility with a B2B audience that does exactly this kind of due diligence before signing a contract.

The tell here usually isn't visual — modern models can produce faces that pass a casual glance completely. The tell is contextual: a "Meet Our Team" page where every person has flawless, symmetrical features and slightly-too-perfect studio lighting is a pattern experienced buyers have learned to recognize, even if they can't articulate why in the moment.

Hands, text, and physical interaction

This is improving fast, but it's still the most reliable technical tell. Hands holding objects, people typing on a specific keyboard layout, anyone interacting with a product in a way that requires precise physical logic — fingers wrapped around a coffee cup, a hand actually gripping a steering wheel — these scenarios still produce artifacts often enough that I treat them as high-risk. Even when the hand itself renders correctly, the object it's supposedly holding frequently doesn't obey physics: a phone screen at the wrong angle for how it's being held, a pen positioned in a grip no human would use to write.

Text within images is the other classic tell. Signage, product labels, book spines, screen content — diffusion models have gotten dramatically better at rendering legible text, but "dramatically better" still means an unpredictable failure rate, and one garbled word on a laptop screen in your hero image is enough to make a technically sophisticated visitor distrust the whole page.

Products, especially your client's actual product

AI cannot photograph a product that doesn't exist as training data in a form the model actually understands. If a client sells a specific physical product — a piece of furniture, a skincare bottle with a particular label design, a device with a specific port layout — AI generation will approximate it based on similar products it has seen, and the approximation is never exact. This matters enormously for e-commerce and product marketing, where the entire point of the image is to communicate exactly what the customer is buying. I would never recommend AI imagery for actual product shots. Product photography or 3D rendering from the actual CAD files are the only trustworthy options here, because the cost of a customer receiving something different from what they saw is far higher than the cost of the photography.

Specific places, especially the client's own location

A restaurant's actual dining room, a clinic's actual waiting area, a hotel's actual lobby — these need to be real, because customers will show up expecting the space they saw online. I've seen this mistake made by well-meaning junior designers who generated a "cozy, modern café interior" for a client's homepage because the client hadn't sent good photos yet, and it went live as a placeholder that nobody remembered to replace. The café's actual interior looked nothing like it. That's not an aesthetic problem, it's a false-advertising problem, and it's the kind of thing that generates one-star reviews mentioning "the website lied about the vibe."

Emotionally loaded or identity-sensitive contexts

Healthcare, therapy, grief, disability representation, cultural or religious imagery — these categories require a level of nuance and lived specificity that generative models, trained on averaged internet imagery, tend to flatten into stereotype. AI-generated images in these contexts often produce a kind of generic, slightly-off representation that reads as tone-deaf even when no single element is technically wrong. A wheelchair rendered with subtly incorrect mechanics, a religious garment styled slightly wrong, a grief scenario staged with an expression that reads as posed rather than felt — these are the images that generate complaints, not because anyone can point to an artifact, but because something about them feels hollow. Stock photography has its own version of this problem, honestly, but at least professional stock photographers are increasingly working with real models from the communities being represented. AI averages across whatever the training data contained, which is not the same thing.

Anything at scale where consistency of a real subject matters

If a campaign needs the "same person" across multiple images — a brand ambassador, a recurring character in an explainer series, a spokesperson across a video and print campaign — most AI tools still struggle to maintain perfect facial consistency across generations without specific fine-tuning workflows that add significant time and cost. At that point you're often better off either hiring a real model for a proper shoot or using a much more deliberate AI pipeline (LoRA training on a consistent character, careful seed management) that starts to cost nearly as much as photography anyway, just with different risk.

The specific visual tells that give AI away

It's worth being concrete about what "looks fake" actually means technically, because vague pattern-matching ("something feels off") isn't useful feedback during a design review. Here's what I actually check for when evaluating whether an AI image is safe to ship:

  • Texture uniformity. Real skin, fabric, and materials have irregularities — pores, fiber variation, small imperfections. AI images, especially at default settings, tend toward suspiciously smooth, evenly-lit surfaces. This is the single biggest subconscious tell for viewers, even ones who couldn't name it.
  • Background coherence. Check what's happening at the edges and in the blurred background. Furniture that melts into itself, a window with impossible geometry, a second face partially formed in a crowd behind the subject. Models are much better at the focal subject than the periphery.
  • Light source consistency. Does every shadow agree on where the light is coming from? Real photography enforces this by physics. AI generation frequently produces plausible-looking lighting on the subject that contradicts the lighting implied by the background.
  • Repetition and symmetry. Jewelry, buttons, patterns on fabric, architectural details — look for elements that repeat with suspicious perfection or, conversely, dissolve into abstract mush past a certain point in the frame.
  • Emotional flatness. This one is less technical and more perceptual, but it's real: AI-generated expressions often land in an uncanny middle ground — technically a smile, but missing the specific asymmetries and micro-expressions that make a real human smile read as genuine on screen.
  • Context mismatches. An office scene with a calendar showing no legible dates, a "handwritten" note that isn't quite readable, a screen displaying UI that resembles software but isn't functional software. These details don't register consciously for most viewers, but they contribute to the low-grade sense that something is staged rather than documented.

The useful exercise for any design team is to build an internal checklist like this and actually run new AI assets through it before they ship, rather than relying on "does it look okay" during a quick Slack approval. Most of the embarrassing AI-image failures I've seen published by real companies would have been caught by a five-minute check against a list exactly like this one.

A workflow for making AI imagery believable when it needs to pass

When AI imagery is the right call but the bar for realism is higher than "abstract background," there's a workflow that meaningfully improves results. This isn't about better prompting alone — it's about treating AI generation as the first step in a pipeline, not the final deliverable.

Start with composition, not description

Most weak AI images come from prompts that describe content ("a woman using a laptop in a coffee shop") without describing photographic construction. Specifying focal length, lighting setup, camera angle, and film or lens characteristics ("shot on 50mm, natural window light, shallow depth of field, slight motion blur on the hand") pushes the model toward photographic logic rather than illustrative averaging. This alone eliminates a large percentage of the "obviously generated" quality, because it constrains the model toward outputs that respect actual optics.

Generate in volume, curate hard

The realistic workflow is never "generate one image and ship it." It's generating fifteen to thirty variations and rejecting all but one or two. This is the part clients underestimate when they assume AI imagery is instant — the generation is instant, the curation is not. A designer who understands the tells above needs to look at every candidate and reject anything with a background artifact, an inconsistent light source, or an expression that reads as hollow. Budget the time for this the same way you'd budget time for photo selection from a real shoot.

Composite and correct in post

Raw AI output rarely goes straight to production in serious work. Standard retouching passes — color grading to match the brand palette, subtle texture addition to reduce the too-smooth skin/material problem, cropping to hide a weak background element, sometimes compositing a real photographed element (a real product, a real logo on a screen) into an AI-generated scene — close most of the remaining gap. Treat the AI output as a base plate, not a finished asset.

Match resolution and grain to its context

A crisp, hyper-detailed AI image dropped next to genuinely photographed content on the same page creates a visual seam that draws attention to itself even when neither image is individually suspicious. If the rest of a site's photography has a particular grain, color temperature, or compression characteristic, AI-generated images need to be processed to match, or they'll read as "the odd one out" even to viewers who can't say why.

Test with people who weren't in the room

The team that generated and approved an image is the worst possible judge of whether it looks fake, because they've seen thirty rejected variations and have become anchored to the one that seemed best by comparison, not by absolute standard. Before shipping anything where realism matters — a testimonial section, a hero image meant to represent real customers — show it cold to someone outside the project and ask directly: "does anything about this look off to you?" This single step catches more problems than any amount of internal review.

A creative director I collaborated with on a healthcare rebrand put it well during a review: "The goal isn't to make an image that survives a Photoshop-forensics Twitter thread. The goal is to make an image that doesn't make our actual audience feel lied to."

That distinction is the right north star. You are not trying to defeat a forensic examination. You are trying to avoid the specific, ordinary moment where a real visitor's gut says "something about this isn't real" and their trust in the rest of the page quietly drops a notch, even if they never consciously articulate why.

The client conversation: expectations, ethics, and disclosure

Part of this decision isn't purely visual — it's a conversation you need to have with the client up front, because the risk isn't evenly distributed between designer and client, and a lot of the bad outcomes I've seen came from a client assuming AI imagery was a free, consequence-free swap for stock photography without understanding the specific risks.

Disclosure norms are shifting fast

Several jurisdictions have started introducing disclosure requirements for AI-generated advertising imagery, and platforms including major ad networks and social media companies have added labeling requirements for synthetic media. This is a moving target, and it's worth telling clients explicitly: what's acceptable without disclosure today may require a label next year. Building projects with an assumption of permanent, disclosure-free AI use is a bet against a regulatory trend that is currently moving in one direction.

Rights and training data still matter to some clients

Some clients — particularly in creative industries, publishing, or anywhere with a unionized workforce touching the project — have real concerns about the training data behind image models and won't want AI imagery used at all, on principle, regardless of quality. This isn't a technical objection and no amount of "but it looks realistic" will resolve it. It's worth asking directly, early in a project, whether the client has a position on AI-generated imagery before you build a deck full of it and present it as a fait accompli.

Set the realism bar explicitly, in writing

I've started including a short line in project scoping documents that specifies where AI imagery is approved for use and where it isn't — background and conceptual assets: yes; testimonial or team photography: no, real photography only; product imagery: no, product photography or verified 3D renders only. This isn't bureaucratic overhead, it's the single cheapest way to prevent the scenario where a junior team member fills a "team page" placeholder with AI faces that never get flagged before launch.

Budget for the curation time, not just the generation cost

Clients hear "AI image" and mentally price it at zero, because the generation itself often is close to free. The actual cost is designer time: prompting, generating in volume, checking against the visual-tell checklist, retouching, and testing with outside eyes. Quoting a project as if AI imagery is a free line item, then discovering the curation work takes as long as sourcing good stock photography would have, is a fast way to erode margin on a fixed-price project. Price the workflow, not the tool.

A practical decision framework

After enough of these projects, the decision usually comes down to answering a short set of questions honestly before generating anything:

  1. Does this image claim to represent something specific and real — a real person, a real product, a real place? If yes, AI is high-risk regardless of quality, and real photography or verified assets are the safer default.
  2. Will anyone scrutinize this closely, or is it glanced at once? Hero images, testimonials, and product pages get scrutinized. Blog headers and background textures don't. Match the effort to the attention the image will actually receive.
  3. Does the category involve hands, legible text, or precise physical interaction? If yes, budget extra curation time or avoid AI for that specific frame, even if the rest of the page uses it fine.
  4. Is the subject matter emotionally or culturally sensitive? If yes, lean toward real photography and real representation, or at minimum have someone from the relevant community review the output before it ships.
  5. Does the client have an explicit position on AI imagery, disclosure, or training-data ethics? Ask before you build, not after you present.
  6. Can this image be tested cold with someone outside the project before launch? If the answer is no because of timeline pressure, that's itself a signal the asset is being rushed past the one check that catches most failures.

None of this is really about AI versus stock as a philosophical debate. It's about matching the tool to how much trust the image is being asked to carry. A gradient background carries almost no trust burden — nobody's confidence in your client's business rests on whether the hero section's abstract shape was generated or hand-illustrated. A "Meet the Team" photo carries an enormous trust burden, because it's implicitly a promise: these are the real people you'll be working with. Stock photography made a version of this mistake for years, and audiences developed pattern recognition for it. AI imagery is capable of a more convincing version of the same mistake, and the audiences developing pattern recognition for it are catching up fast. The teams that get this right aren't the ones with the best prompting skills — they're the ones asking, honestly, whether the specific image in front of them needs to be true.
