What I Put in My Client Contract After 400 Projects

Oct 1, 2026 · 19 min read

The Contract Is a Scar Tissue Document

My first contract was two pages long. It said, in essence, "I will design things, you will pay me, here's roughly when." It was optimistic in the way that only someone who has never been burned can be optimistic. Somewhere around project 40, a client asked for "just a few tweaks" that turned into a six-week redesign with no additional payment. Around project 90, someone disappeared for four months mid-project and then resurfaced expecting me to pick up exactly where I left off, at the same price, on the same timeline. Around project 150, a client took my Figma files, handed them to an in-house team, and launched a product that looked suspiciously like my unreleased concept — before I'd been paid the final invoice.

Every one of those situations taught me something, and the contract I use today is really just scar tissue arranged into paragraphs. It's not a legal document designed to intimidate anyone. It's a record of every way a project has gone sideways on me, written down so it doesn't happen the same way twice.

I say this upfront because I think a lot of designers approach contracts backward. They download a template, swap in their name and rate, and call it done. Templates are a fine starting point, but they're built for a hypothetical average client, and your actual clients are never average. After 400 projects — some smooth, some genuinely painful — I've landed on a specific set of clauses that aren't in most boilerplate, because most boilerplate was never in the room when things actually broke. This article walks through what's in my contract now, and more importantly, the exact situation that put each clause there.

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice — every jurisdiction and client relationship is different, and you should have an actual attorney review anything you send out. But I've learned that a contract written from lived experience, even an imperfect one, protects you more than a perfect one you don't understand.

Scope Has to Be a List, Not a Paragraph

The single biggest source of conflict in 400 projects has never been money. It's scope. Specifically, it's the gap between what a client thinks they're paying for and what a designer thinks they're delivering. Early in my career, my contracts described scope in prose: "Design of a responsive marketing website including homepage, about page, and contact page." That sentence feels precise until a client asks, reasonably from their perspective, "what about the pricing page? That's part of the marketing site." Prose scope is a negotiation waiting to happen, because every noun in it can be reinterpreted.

Now my contracts include scope as a literal numbered list, broken down to the screen or component level, with explicit exclusions. For a website project, that looks like:

  • Homepage (desktop and mobile breakpoints, 1 layout direction with 2 rounds of feedback)
  • About page (desktop and mobile)
  • Pricing page (desktop and mobile, up to 3 pricing tiers displayed)
  • Contact page (desktop and mobile)
  • Navigation and footer components, applied consistently across the above
  • Does not include: blog templates, dashboard or logged-in states, email templates, print collateral, or additional pages beyond those listed above

That last line — the explicit exclusion — is the part most contracts skip, and it's the part that's saved me the most grief. Clients don't maliciously expand scope most of the time; they genuinely don't know where the boundary is unless you draw it for them. Writing "does not include" forces a conversation before the project starts, not an argument in week six.

I also learned to put a number on "rounds of feedback" per deliverable, not per project. A client once interpreted "two rounds of revisions" as two rounds total across an entire twelve-screen app, which meant a single round of feedback per screen was essentially impossible to execute well — they'd use all their revisions on the first three screens debating button color, then expect unlimited changes on the rest because "we hadn't gotten to use our revisions yet." Now the contract specifies revisions per deliverable: two rounds of feedback per screen or component, with anything beyond that billed at the hourly change-order rate described later. It's more words on the page, but it removes an entire category of disagreement.

The other scope lesson came from a project where a client's "quick logo refresh" conversation during a kickoff call somehow became an expectation that I'd also redo their business cards, email signature, and social media templates, because "while you're in there, might as well." None of that was in the signed scope. I hadn't said no clearly, and the client hadn't maliciously assumed — the ambiguity of a verbal conversation had just quietly expanded the project. Now there's a line in the contract: "Any deliverables not explicitly listed in the Scope section above are considered out of scope, regardless of discussion during meetings or calls, and will be quoted separately." It sounds almost aggressive written out like that, but in four years of using that exact sentence, no client has ever objected to it. The ones who push back on a sentence like that are, frankly, the ones you want to identify before the deposit clears.

Change Orders Turn "Can You Just" Into Paid Work

Scope lists solve the initial boundary problem, but they don't solve what happens after the project is underway and new, legitimate needs appear. A client discovers mid-project that they also need a checkout flow, or decides the three-tier pricing page actually needs to support five tiers, or the stakeholder who wasn't in the kickoff call suddenly has opinions. These aren't bad-faith scope creep — they're normal business evolution. But normal business evolution still needs to be paid for, and it needs a mechanism that doesn't require an awkward renegotiation conversation every single time.

That mechanism is a change order clause, and it's one of the more underused tools in freelance contracts. Mine reads roughly like this: any request that falls outside the defined scope will be documented as a change order, including a brief description of the work, the estimated hours, and the additional cost, and work on that change order will not begin until it's approved in writing (email is fine) by the client. It explicitly states that verbal approval on a call is not sufficient — it has to be in writing, even if that writing is just a one-line "yes, approved" email reply.

I added that writing requirement after a call where a client said "yeah, let's add that" about an extra dashboard view, I did the work, and then at invoicing they said they didn't recall agreeing to anything beyond our original scope. I believe they genuinely didn't remember — it was a fast-moving call with six agenda items — but without anything in writing, I had no leg to stand on, and I ate the cost of that extra view. Now every change order is a short written exchange before I touch Figma, and it's made the entire process faster, not slower, because it removes the ambiguity that used to cause arguments at invoice time.

The other thing I've learned to build into the change order clause is a default hourly rate that kicks in automatically, rather than requiring a fresh quote negotiation every time. If my project rate is fixed-fee, the contract states a standalone hourly rate for anything out of scope — say $150/hour — so that small additions don't require a whole new proposal cycle. A client wanting one extra page doesn't need a three-email negotiation; they get a quick estimate in hours at the pre-agreed rate, approve it, and we move on. This single clause has probably saved more relationships than any other, because it turns "can you just add one more thing" from a tense moment into a two-line email.

Payment Terms That Assume Nothing

I used to invoice 50% up front and 50% on completion. It's the most common structure in the industry, and it's also, I eventually realized, structured to protect the client far more than the designer. "On completion" is a subjective milestone that the client effectively controls — if they decide the work isn't "complete" because of an unresolved dispute, feedback they haven't given yet, or just general slow-walking, that final 50% can sit unpaid indefinitely while you've already done 100% of the work.

After a project where the final payment took five months to collect — despite delivered, approved, implemented work — I restructured payment entirely around milestones tied to deliverables, not to vague completion states, and I front-loaded the percentages. For a typical project now:

  • 30% deposit due before any work begins, non-refundable once work has started
  • 30% due upon delivery of initial concepts/wireframes, before moving to high-fidelity design
  • 30% due upon delivery of final high-fidelity designs, before handoff files are released
  • 10% due upon final handoff and project closeout

Two things matter in that structure beyond the percentages. First, each payment is tied to a specific, unambiguous deliverable, not a feeling of "doneness." Second, I don't release the next phase of work until the prior payment clears. That second part used to feel confrontational to enforce, but I've found that clients who are going to pay reliably don't blink at it, and clients who do blink at it are usually the ones who were going to be a problem later anyway. It's an early filter, not just a cash-flow tool.

I also added a specific clause about late payment that has nothing to do with legal threats and everything to do with simple mechanics: invoices unpaid after 15 days accrue a 1.5% monthly late fee, and work pauses entirely on any invoice unpaid after 10 days until payment is received. The pause clause matters more than the late fee — the late fee is rarely worth collecting in small amounts, but the automatic pause means I'm never in a position where I'm doing week four of unpaid work hoping week two's invoice eventually clears. The contract makes the pause automatic and pre-agreed, so invoking it isn't a confrontation; it's just what the document already said would happen.

Finally, there's a kill fee clause for projects that end early, for any reason — client budget cuts, change in direction, company restructuring, whatever. If a project is terminated after the deposit and before completion, the client pays for all completed milestones plus 20% of the next milestone's value, to account for the work of pausing, documenting handoff, and the opportunity cost of the slot I held for them. Before this clause existed, early terminations meant I absorbed the cost of a half-finished project and the empty calendar slot I'd already planned around. Now that risk is priced in from day one, and in practice, it's rarely invoked — but having it written down has made conversations about pausing or ending projects early far less tense, because nobody has to negotiate fairness in the moment; the fairness was already agreed to before anyone was upset.

Defining "Done" So Revisions Don't Become Infinite

"Unlimited revisions" is a phrase I see on other designers' sites sometimes, usually meant as a selling point, and it is, in my experience, one of the most dangerous promises a designer can make. Not because clients are inherently unreasonable, but because without a defined endpoint, there is no natural mechanism that pushes a project toward completion. I once worked with a client on a logo project with "unlimited revisions" baked into an early pricing package, and we went through 14 rounds of concepts over four months on a project that should have taken three weeks, because every round surfaced a new stakeholder opinion and there was no contractual reason for the process to stop. Every subsequent contract has specified an exact number of revision rounds per deliverable, what counts as a "round," and what happens after those rounds are exhausted.

A round, in my contract's definition, is a single batch of consolidated feedback delivered at one time, which I then address in one updated version. This definition matters because clients will sometimes send feedback in five separate messages over three days, each with new notes — "oh, also, can we try the blue a bit darker" arriving two days after the "approved, looks great" message. Without a clear definition, every one of those messages could be argued to be its own "round." My contract states explicitly that feedback must be consolidated and delivered as a single round within a defined window (I use five business days), and that feedback arriving after that window, or arriving piecemeal, counts toward the next round rather than resetting the clock on the current one.

I also added a specific definition of what happens when revision rounds run out: additional rounds are billed at the standard hourly change-order rate, and are optional — the client can also simply accept the current version as final. This removes any ambiguity about whether I'm obligated to keep iterating for free past the agreed number, and it removes the awkwardness of me having to bring up payment mid-revision; the contract already settled it.

One more addition here, which came out of a project where a client kept requesting "just one more small tweak" on a logo for what became eleven separate "small tweaks": the contract now defines a revision round as covering changes within the same creative direction, and specifies that a request for an entirely new creative direction (not a refinement of the approved direction) counts as a new deliverable requiring its own scope and payment discussion. This distinction — refinement versus new direction — has been genuinely useful in conversations, because it gives both of us shared vocabulary. I can say "this feels like a new direction rather than a refinement of what we approved, so let's scope it as an addition" instead of just feeling stuck and absorbing the extra work silently, which is what I used to do.

Intellectual Property Doesn't Transfer Until the Invoice Clears

This is the clause that came from the worst single experience in 400 projects, so I'll describe it plainly. I delivered final high-fidelity files for an app redesign, the client said they loved it, and then went quiet for three weeks on the final invoice. During that silence, the app launched — with my designs, essentially unmodified — built by an internal team the client had apparently brought on partway through the project without mentioning it. I eventually got paid, after an uncomfortable series of emails, but the experience exposed a gap in every contract I'd used up to that point: none of them actually stated when ownership of the design work transferred to the client. Most freelance designers assume ownership transfers on delivery. It doesn't, unless the contract says so — and even when it does, "delivery" is a fuzzy enough term that it's worth nailing down exactly.

My contract now states explicitly that all intellectual property rights in the deliverables — including source files, exported assets, and any associated design systems — remain with the designer until full and final payment has been received, at which point rights transfer fully to the client. Before final payment, the client receives a limited license to view and discuss the work for feedback purposes only, not to implement, publish, or hand off to a third-party development team. This clause has never actually needed to be enforced through any serious legal action, but it's changed the tone of a couple of slow-payment situations, because I can point to a specific line that says, essentially, "this isn't yours to build yet." That's a very different conversation than chasing an invoice with no leverage behind it.

I also added a clause specifically addressing what happens to work-in-progress files if a project is terminated early: incomplete deliverables remain the property of the designer and are not required to be handed over unless the client has paid for the milestone covering that specific work. This came from a situation where a project was cancelled halfway through a design system build, and the client asked for the half-finished Figma file "just to see where it was at," which I initially handed over out of good faith — and then didn't hear from them again, despite an unpaid milestone invoice. Half-finished work has real value; handing it over for free because a project ended awkwardly isn't something I do anymore, and the contract makes that explicit rather than leaving it to an in-the-moment judgment call.

On the flip side — and this matters for trust, not just protection — I also explicitly state what the client DOES get once final payment clears: full ownership of final deliverables, usage rights with no restriction on how they use the work commercially, and source files in editable format (not just exported PDFs or PNGs). Early in my career I was vague about file formats, and one client assumed "deliverables" meant final exported images, not editable source files, and was upset to learn they'd need to pay an additional fee to get the actual Figma file. Being explicit about exactly what transfers, and in what format, prevents that kind of disappointment from happening at the worst possible moment — after the project is technically over and the relationship is winding down.

Communication Boundaries Protect the Work, Not Just My Time

This is the newest category of clause in my contract, added only in the last couple of years, and it came from a slower, more insidious problem than the dramatic ones above: scope creep and burnout caused not by any single bad event, but by an accumulation of always-on availability expectations. A client would message at 9pm "just a quick question," then another at 7am, then a Slack thread with four people tagging me across a weekend about a decision that could have waited until Monday. None of it was malicious. It was just that nothing in writing had ever established what responsiveness was reasonable to expect, so the expectation quietly calibrated itself to whatever I was willing to tolerate — which, because I wanted to be seen as responsive and easy to work with, kept expanding.

My contract now includes a short section on communication expectations: primary communication happens through one agreed channel (email or a project management tool, not personal phone number or personal social media), response time is one business day for non-urgent requests, and work is not conducted on weekends or after standard business hours unless pre-arranged and billed as rush work at a premium rate. It sounds almost petty to put in a legal document, but I've found that writing it down, upfront, before any relationship dynamics have formed, makes it a neutral policy rather than a personal boundary I'm asserting mid-project when tensions are already a little raised. Nobody has ever pushed back on this clause at signing. A few clients have tested it a little in practice — a late-night message here or there — but having the written agreement to point back to has made it easy to respond the next business day without guilt or explanation.

I also added a meeting cadence clause, limiting the number of included calls per project phase (I use two per milestone: a kickoff and a review) with additional meetings beyond that billed at the hourly rate or requiring a scheduling conversation. This came from a project with a client who scheduled calls almost daily "just to check in," each 30 to 45 minutes, none of which were technically unreasonable requests individually, but which collectively consumed an amount of time that was never priced into the original quote. Meetings feel like "free" communication to clients in a way that design hours obviously don't, but they cost real time and attention, and the contract now reflects that.

The underlying principle behind this whole section is one I wish I'd understood earlier: clients aren't being unreasonable when they test the edges of availability, because most of them have never worked with a freelancer before and have no sense of what's standard. The contract's job isn't to shame them for asking; it's to answer the question before they have to ask it, so the boundary is established by a neutral document rather than by me, personally, saying no in real time.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Their 1st Project, Not Their 400th

If you're newer to freelance design and reading this thinking you need all of this on day one, you don't, and trying to front-load a 400-project contract onto your very first client is probably counterproductive — it'll read as adversarial from someone who hasn't yet earned the standing to seem that cautious, and it'll slow down deals you actually need to close. The honest version of this advice is that the contract should grow exactly as fast as your scar tissue does, and not faster.

What I'd actually prioritize, if I were rebuilding from zero: get the scope list and exclusions right first, because that single habit prevents more disputes than every other clause combined. Get the payment milestone structure right second, because cash flow problems compound and distract from the actual design work faster than almost anything else. Everything else — the IP transfer timing, the revision round definitions, the communication boundaries — can be added as you encounter the specific situation that makes you wish you'd had it. You will encounter that situation. Everyone does. The only question is whether you write the clause down afterward or just quietly resent that client type forever.

The other thing I'd say, honestly, is that a contract this specific isn't primarily a legal shield — in four years of using something close to this version, I've never actually needed to pursue legal action over any of these clauses. Its real value is that it does the uncomfortable conversations in advance, in neutral written language, before any money or relationship is on the line. Nobody feels defensive reading a scope exclusion list during onboarding. Everybody feels defensive hearing "that's actually outside what we agreed to" for the first time in week six, from a person they're currently annoyed at. The contract just moves every hard conversation to the one moment in the relationship where it's cheapest to have it — the beginning, before anyone's invested, annoyed, or waiting on a deliverable.

Four hundred projects in, my contract is nine pages long, and it keeps growing a little every year. That's not a sign that client relationships are getting worse. It's a sign that I keep encountering new, specific ways things can go sideways, and I'd rather write each one down once than relearn it twice.
