Dark Mode: Real Need or Just a Design Trend?

Oct 11, 2026 · 17 min read

Why every client asks for dark mode now

At some point in the last five years, dark mode stopped being a developer preference and became a line item in nearly every project brief I receive. It doesn't matter if the client runs a dental clinic's booking site or a B2B analytics platform — somewhere in the kickoff call, someone says, "Oh, and we'd love a dark mode option too." Usually it's phrased as an afterthought, like asking for a favicon.

I understand where it comes from. Apple and Google both shipped system-wide dark themes around 2019, Twitter and YouTube made it a visible toggle, and suddenly dark mode became a signal of a product being modern, technical, and well-maintained. It got attached to a kind of design literacy — if your app doesn't have it, maybe you haven't updated anything in a while. That perception is powerful, and it's not entirely wrong. But it's also not a reason to build one.

Here's the uncomfortable truth I tell clients, sometimes to their visible disappointment: dark mode is not a universal upgrade. It is a feature with real costs — design costs, engineering costs, QA costs, and ongoing maintenance costs — and like any feature, it needs a job to do. Sometimes that job is obvious and valuable. Sometimes it's decorative. The rest of this article is about telling the difference, because I've watched teams spend three or four weeks building a dark theme that less than 2% of users ever switch to, while actual usability problems in the light interface sat untouched.

This isn't an argument against dark mode. I've shipped it dozens of times and I think it's genuinely great in the right context. It's an argument for treating it as a design decision with criteria, not a trend you adopt because the bigger players did.

The cases where dark mode is genuinely the right call

Let's start with where I say yes immediately, no debate needed, because the use case justifies the cost on its own.

Low-light, extended-use tools

If people use your product in dim environments for long stretches — code editors, video editing suites, music production tools, security camera dashboards, night-shift operations software — dark mode isn't a preference, it's an ergonomic requirement. A radiologist reviewing scans at 2am, a sound engineer in a dark studio, a trader with six monitors in a low-lit office: these are people for whom a bright white interface is genuinely uncomfortable and fatiguing over a full shift. I worked with a logistics company whose dispatch software ran on wall-mounted monitors in a dimly lit control room 24/7. Light mode there wasn't just disliked, it was functionally a problem — glare bouncing off the glass, operators squinting. That's a legitimate, measurable use case.

Media and content-heavy interfaces

Apps centered on photography, video, or any visually rich content benefit from dark surroundings because dark neutral backgrounds make color and contrast in the content pop, the same reason photo galleries and cinemas are dark. Lightroom, VSCO, most streaming platforms — their dark UI isn't about battery life or eye strain, it's about not competing with the content for visual attention. If your product's core value is displaying rich visual media, dark mode supports the actual job of the interface: getting out of the way.

Developer and technical tools

This audience has a strong, well-established preference, close to an expectation. If you're building an admin panel, API documentation site, or a CLI companion dashboard for developers, skipping dark mode will actually register as a gap, because this specific audience has normalized it across their entire toolchain — their terminal, their IDE, their browser devtools. It's less about objective ergonomics here and more about matching the expected environment of the user. Context matters more than universal rules.

OLED-heavy mobile usage with long session times

For mobile apps with genuinely long session durations — reading apps, messaging apps people leave open for hours, note-taking tools — dark mode on OLED screens provides a real, measurable battery benefit, because black pixels are actually off rather than lit. This is one of the few places where "trendy" and "technically beneficial" overlap cleanly. If your app's usage pattern involves people staring at it for long stretches on phones, this is a legitimate efficiency argument, not just aesthetics.

Accessibility for specific visual conditions

Some users with light sensitivity (certain migraine conditions, some forms of photophobia, certain stages of cataracts) genuinely find bright white interfaces painful rather than merely less preferred. This is a smaller slice of your audience than marketing copy about dark mode usually implies, but it's real, and if your product serves a population where this matters — health platforms, assistive tools — it's worth the investment on inclusion grounds alone.

Notice the pattern across all five of these: in every case, there's a specific, nameable reason tied to how and where people actually use the product. None of them are "because dark mode looks modern." That distinction is the whole point of this article.

Where dark mode is mostly a trend, not a need

Now the harder conversations — the ones I actually have most often, because most of my clients don't run night-shift control rooms or video editing suites. They run marketing sites, e-commerce stores, SaaS dashboards used during business hours, and internal tools.

Marketing and brand-driven websites

A marketing site's job is to communicate brand, build trust, and move someone toward a decision in a short session. Dark mode here is almost always an aesthetic choice masquerading as a feature. I've had clients request it because a competitor has it, and when I ask "what problem does it solve for your visitors," the honest answer is usually "it looks sleek." That's a legitimate design taste, and if the brand genuinely works in dark (a nightlife venue, a gaming studio, a premium audio brand), build it as the primary and only theme — a deliberate dark brand identity, not a toggle. What I push back on is the toggle itself, maintaining two full themes for a five-minute visitor session, when that engineering time could go toward conversion-focused work like page speed or clearer CTAs.

E-commerce storefronts

Product photography is shot against controlled backgrounds, typically white or neutral, because that's how color accuracy and consistency are maintained across a catalog. Flip the storefront into dark mode and product images — which weren't designed for dark surrounds — can look muddy, color-shifted, or like they're floating in void with harsh white halos. I've seen stores ship dark mode and then quietly disable it on product pages because the product photography just didn't hold up, which tells you something about the actual cost-benefit here. Unless your entire catalog is shot and retouched specifically for a dark context, this is a trap.

Business-hours SaaS dashboards with short average sessions

If your tool is something people open a handful of times a day for a few minutes — a billing dashboard, a CRM glanced at between meetings, a scheduling tool — the ergonomic argument for dark mode weakens considerably. The sessions are short, they're happening in normal office lighting, and the benefit of dark mode (reduced eye strain over sustained low-light use) simply doesn't apply. I'm not saying don't build it; plenty of users will still ask for it and like having it. I'm saying don't treat it as urgent or load-bearing for the product's success, and don't let it eat the roadmap budget for actual workflow improvements.

"Because the competitor has it" requests

This is probably the single most common reason I hear, and it's the weakest one. Competitive feature-matching makes sense when the feature is solving a real, shared user problem that your competitor identified before you did. It makes much less sense when the feature is a surface-level trend that a competitor adopted for the same reasons you're considering it — because everyone else was doing it. I ask clients one question here: if your competitor didn't have dark mode, would you still want to build it? If the honest answer is no, that's useful information.

None of this means dark mode is bad in these contexts. It means the decision should be made on its actual merits for that specific product and audience, not on ambient industry pressure. The word "trend" isn't an insult — trends exist because something resonated — but a trend is not the same thing as a requirement, and conflating the two is how teams end up maintaining features nobody benefits from.

What a real dark mode implementation actually costs

Clients frequently underestimate this, and I think it's because the end result — a toggle switch — looks so simple. The switch is simple. Everything behind it isn't. Here's what "add dark mode" actually requires if done properly rather than as a quick CSS invert.

  • A full second set of color tokens, not an inversion. Flipping black to white and white to black produces a harsh, often broken result. Good dark themes use desaturated, warmer dark grays rather than pure black, and need their own carefully chosen accent colors, because colors that read as vibrant on white can look neon or vibrate uncomfortably on dark backgrounds. This is a genuine color system redesign, not a filter.
  • Re-checked contrast ratios across every text/background pairing. A color combination that passes WCAG AA in light mode can fail in dark mode, and vice versa, because contrast math isn't symmetrical in the way people assume. Every button state, form error, disabled state, and badge needs re-verification.
  • Illustrations, icons, and charts redesigned or adapted. Flat-color illustrations built for white backgrounds often have thin outlines or color choices that disappear or clash in dark mode. Data visualizations need a separate palette, because categorical colors that are distinguishable on white may not be distinguishable on dark gray, especially for colorblind users.
  • Shadows and elevation systems rebuilt. Drop shadows, which communicate elevation and hierarchy in light UI, barely register against dark backgrounds. Dark mode design systems typically replace shadow-based elevation with subtle background lightness steps or thin borders instead — a structurally different approach, not a copy-paste.
  • Photography and media treatment decisions. As mentioned above, real photos often need framing, padding, or subtle background treatment to sit well in a dark context.
  • QA across every screen state, not just the happy path. Empty states, error states, loading skeletons, modals, tooltips, hover states — every one of these needs visual verification in both themes. This roughly doubles visual QA time, and it's the part most project timelines quietly forget to budget for.
  • Ongoing maintenance tax. Every new feature shipped afterward needs to be designed and tested in both themes forever. This is the cost that compounds — not the initial build, but the permanent doubling of your design and QA surface area for every future change.

I ask clients to picture this list, specifically the last item, before approving a dark mode toggle. The initial build is a project. The maintenance is a permanent tax on every future project. If the usage data or the use case doesn't justify a one-time cost, it almost certainly doesn't justify an ongoing one.

How to actually decide — the questions I ask clients

Instead of starting from "do you want dark mode," I've found it far more productive to start from questions about the product and its users, and let the answer fall out naturally. Here's roughly the sequence I walk through on a discovery call.

  1. Where and when is this product typically used? If the honest answer involves phrases like "late at night," "dimly lit environment," or "for hours at a stretch," that's a point toward building it. If the answer is "during the workday, in an office," it's a point against urgency.
  2. What does usage data actually say, if you have it? If you already have a live product, check how many users have their OS set to dark mode and are visiting your site with no dedicated theme — most analytics and some CMS setups can surface this via the prefers-color-scheme media feature. I've had clients discover that fewer than 8% of their traffic runs in dark mode system-wide, which reframes the "everyone wants this" assumption immediately. I've also had the opposite — a developer tool where north of 60% of visits came in on dark-mode systems, which made the business case for themselves.
  3. Is the product's core value visual/media-based? Photography, video, portfolios — leans toward dark mode supporting the content rather than competing with it. Forms, text-heavy information, checkout flows — leans away.
  4. What's the actual session length? Five-minute glances don't accumulate eye strain. Ninety-minute working sessions do. This single factor probably predicts user benefit better than any other.
  5. Who, specifically, is asking for it? Sometimes it's one vocal power user or a single stakeholder's personal preference, not a broad pattern. I've traced "our users keep asking for dark mode" back to two support tickets and the founder's own habit of browsing everything in dark mode on his phone. That's useful information, but it's not the same thing as demand.
  6. Can you afford the permanent maintenance cost, not just the build? If the team is lean and the roadmap is already tight, committing to double-theme QA forever is a real tradeoff against other priorities, and it should be weighed as one.
  7. Does your brand identity actually work in dark? Some color systems and photography styles were built for light backgrounds and genuinely can't be ported without a from-scratch palette exercise. If brand doesn't translate, that's added cost on top of everything else.

Running through these questions out loud with a client usually resolves the decision faster than any amount of back-and-forth about whether dark mode is "worth it" in the abstract. The abstract version of this question has no answer. The specific version, applied to their actual product and audience, almost always does.

Doing it properly when the answer is yes

When the use case genuinely justifies it, here's the process I follow to make sure the investment pays off rather than producing a half-finished second theme that quietly embarrasses the brand six months later.

Build the token system first, not the screens

Before touching any individual screen, define a semantic color token system — background-primary, background-elevated, text-primary, text-secondary, border-subtle, accent, destructive, and so on — with values assigned separately for light and dark. Every component in the design system should reference these tokens rather than hard-coded hex values. This is the difference between a maintainable dark mode and a one-off hack: if a new screen is built correctly against tokens, it gets dark mode support automatically, with zero additional design decisions required.

Design the dark palette on its own terms

Resist the instinct to simply invert the light palette. Start from a near-black but not pure-black base (something like a very dark charcoal or deep navy reads as more premium and less harsh than true black, and reduces the "halation" effect where bright text appears to glow or smear against pure black, which is a genuine readability issue for some users). Desaturate and slightly brighten accent colors so they maintain visual weight and contrast without becoming neon. Treat this as its own small design project, with its own review and approval, not a filter pass.

Rebuild elevation without shadows

Use incremental lightness steps for surfaces that sit "above" the base background — cards, modals, dropdowns — rather than shadows, which lose most of their visual effect on dark backgrounds. A clean approach is a defined scale (background, surface-1, surface-2, surface-3) where each step is slightly lighter, communicating depth through tone rather than shadow.

Audit every state, not just default screens

Go through hover, focus, active, disabled, error, success, and empty states individually in both themes. Disabled states in particular are prone to becoming invisible in dark mode because grayed-out text on white already has low contrast, and in dark mode that same relative reduction can drop below legibility entirely.

Treat imagery as a separate workstream

Decide, explicitly, how photography and illustration behave in dark mode: subtle rounded containers with slight padding, a soft inner border, or a light vignette can keep images from looking like floating rectangles against a dark void. For icon sets, check stroke weight and color at both themes — thin single-pixel strokes that look crisp on white can disappear or look fuzzy on dark gray, depending on anti-aliasing.

Test with real content and real users, not lorem ipsum

Dark mode problems tend to hide in edge cases — long text that wraps differently, data tables with alternating row colors, charts with many categories. Test with actual production-like content, and if possible, get a handful of real users who'd genuinely use dark mode to spend real time in it before shipping, rather than relying on the design team's own five-minute glance.

Default intelligently, but always let people choose

Respecting the operating system's prefers-color-scheme setting as the default is good practice — it means your app matches the mode a user has already chosen as a preference across their whole device, which feels respectful rather than presumptuous. But always provide an explicit in-app override too, since plenty of people want their system dark but a specific app light (or vice versa) for the exact ergonomic reasons discussed earlier.

None of these steps are exotic. They're simply what "doing it properly" requires instead of "doing it quickly." The gap between those two is usually what separates a dark mode that feels native and considered from one that feels like an afterthought with inverted colors slapped over the existing layout — and users notice that difference even if they can't articulate exactly what's wrong.

Saying no without sounding like you're against progress

The hardest part of this whole conversation isn't the design work — it's telling a client, diplomatically, that the feature they're excited about isn't the right priority right now. I've found a few things help.

First, I never frame it as "dark mode is bad" or "you don't need it." I frame it as sequencing: this is a good feature, here's the evidence for when it earns its cost, and here's what I'd suggest we prioritize instead given the current data and budget, with dark mode queued as a strong candidate for a later phase once we have usage data to confirm demand. This keeps the client's instinct intact — they're not wrong to think dark mode is valuable in general — while redirecting the immediate resource allocation toward something better supported by their specific situation.

Second, I show rather than tell wherever I can. A quick, rough mockup of their actual product photography or brand colors dropped into a dark background, built in twenty minutes rather than a polished comp, often makes the cost/awkwardness of the mismatch obvious faster than any argument I could make verbally. Seeing their own hero image look washed out or their brand orange turn uncomfortably loud against charcoal tends to recalibrate expectations quickly and without friction, because it's their own content making the case, not my opinion.

Third, I lean on the maintenance framing specifically, because it's the part stakeholders tend to underweight and it's the part that actually matters long-term. Founders and product leads think in terms of the next release; they often don't instinctively price in the fact that every UI decision made from that point forward needs to be checked against two themes forever. Naming that explicitly — "this doubles your design and QA surface for every future feature, indefinitely" — tends to land because it's a concrete, ongoing operational cost rather than an abstract design opinion.

Finally, I keep the door open rather than closing it permanently. Checking prefers-color-scheme analytics after launch, revisiting the decision in three or six months with real usage data, and treating it as a deferred decision rather than a rejected one tends to keep the relationship collaborative. Several of my clients who initially shelved dark mode came back to it later — with actual data in hand — and at that point building it was a much more confident, better-scoped project than it would have been as an impulse add-on during the original build.

Dark mode is a good feature. It's just not a default feature, and treating it as one is how teams end up spending real budget on something that looks impressive in a portfolio screenshot but does nothing for the people actually using the product. The right question was never "should websites have dark mode." It's "does this specific product, for this specific audience, in this specific context of use, actually need it" — and that question has a different answer every time, which is exactly why it's worth asking properly instead of defaulting to whatever the rest of the industry happens to be doing this year.
