5 Signs a Client Isn't Ready to Work With a Designer

Aug 31, 2026 · 16 min read

Why "not ready" isn't an insult

Every designer who has run their own practice for more than a year has a story about a project that should never have started. Not because the client was rude, or the budget was too small, or the brief was ugly — but because the client, as an organization or as a person, wasn't actually prepared to collaborate on design yet. The work stalled, the feedback contradicted itself, three rounds became eleven, and everyone ended up frustrated despite good intentions on both sides.

"Not ready" doesn't mean incompetent, cheap, or difficult. Plenty of smart, well-funded, perfectly reasonable people are not ready to work with a designer at the moment they reach out. They might be ready in three months, after a strategy session, after hiring a product manager, or after a hard conversation with their co-founder. Readiness is a snapshot, not a character trait — which is exactly why it's worth learning to spot it early, before a contract locks you into months of friction.

This matters more for design than for most other services because design sits downstream of decisions that haven't been made yet. A copywriter can work from vague direction and produce something usable. A developer can build against a spec, even an imperfect one. But a designer is usually being asked to give visual and structural form to a business idea — and if that idea hasn't crystallized, no amount of design skill will compensate. You end up designing the client's indecision, one revision at a time.

The five signs below aren't reasons to panic during a discovery call. They're diagnostic. Some of them are fixable in a single conversation. Others tell you the project needs to be postponed, reshaped, or — occasionally — declined. Either way, catching them at the proposal stage is dramatically cheaper than catching them in week six.

Sign 1: They can't describe the problem, only the solution

Ask a ready client "what problem are we solving?" and you get an answer about users, behavior, or business metrics: "New customers get confused during checkout and abandon at a 40% rate." Ask an unready client the same question and you often get a restatement of the request: "We need a new checkout flow." That's not a problem, it's a task. And a task without a problem behind it is a task with no way to measure success.

This shows up constantly in how briefs are written. A ready client's brief, even a rough one, tends to reference cause and effect: what's happening, why it's costly, who's affected. An unready client's brief jumps straight to specification: "5 screens, dark mode, similar to [competitor], needed in three weeks." There's nothing wrong with having references or constraints — that's normal and helpful. The warning sign is the total absence of a "why" underneath the "what."

You'll also hear this in calls when the client answers every question about goals by describing the deliverable instead. Ask "how will we know the redesign worked?" and get "it'll look modern and clean." Ask "what's driving the urgency now?" and get "our competitor just launched something similar." Neither answer is about outcomes. Both are about optics.

"We don't need research, we know what we want — we just need someone to make it look professional."

That sentence, or a close variant of it, is one of the most reliable indicators in this whole list. It signals that the client has already decided design is a cosmetic layer applied at the end of a decision process, rather than a tool for making better decisions in the first place. Sometimes that's fine — a purely visual polish job is a legitimate, bounded engagement. The danger is when the client believes it's a full product or UX engagement, and you believe it too, and six weeks in you discover the "just make it look professional" request actually depends on unresolved questions about user flow, information architecture, or even what the product does.

The fix, when it's fixable, is to reframe the first phase of the engagement explicitly as problem definition rather than production. Offer a short, separately scoped discovery phase — a handful of stakeholder interviews, a review of existing analytics, a one-page problem statement the client has to sign off on before any screens get made. If the client resists paying for or participating in that phase, that itself is diagnostic: they're not resisting your process, they're resisting the idea that there's a problem to define at all. That resistance is worth taking seriously before you start.

Sign 2: There's no real decision-maker in the room

Design decisions get relitigated endlessly when the person approving them isn't the person who can actually approve them. This is the single most expensive readiness gap because it doesn't show up in the kickoff call — it shows up three rounds later, when a design that was "approved" gets vetoed by someone who wasn't in any of the meetings.

Watch for these patterns during scoping:

  • The main point of contact frequently says "let me check with the team" for decisions that should be theirs to make, like color direction or layout preference.
  • Feedback documents arrive as a compiled list from five or six people with no indication of whose opinion carries more weight.
  • The client can't tell you, when asked directly, who has final sign-off — or names someone who then turns out not to attend any calls.
  • Contradictory feedback shows up in the same round: one stakeholder wants it bolder, another wants it calmer, and nobody adjudicates between them.

None of this is inherently a dealbreaker — plenty of good clients work by committee and still ship good products. The difference is whether the committee has a chair. A functioning multi-stakeholder client has someone whose job is to synthesize the group's input into one clear direction before it reaches the designer. A dysfunctional one forwards you the raw, unfiltered opinions of everyone with a Slack account and asks you to reconcile them, which is not a design task, it's a mediation task, and it's not one you're equipped or paid to perform.

This is worth surfacing directly in the proposal stage rather than discovering it live. A simple, low-friction way to do it: ask "when we deliver a design and there's disagreement about direction internally, who makes the final call?" A confident, specific answer — a name and a title — is a good sign. A vague answer ("we'll all discuss it") means you should build a decision-maker requirement into your process before work starts, for instance by asking the client to designate one approver per round, in writing, as a condition of the engagement.

It's also worth distinguishing this from a related but different problem: a decision-maker who exists but is disengaged. Some clients have a clear final approver — a CEO, a founder — who is simply too busy to review work in a timely way, so approvals sit for two weeks at a time while cheaper resources (yours) sit idle or spin on secondary feedback. That's not an authority problem, it's a bandwidth problem, and it's just as capable of sinking a timeline. Ask not just who decides, but how quickly they can realistically turn around a review, and build that into the schedule rather than assuming goodwill will make it happen.

Sign 3: The budget conversation happens before the goals conversation

There's a natural, healthy order to a design engagement conversation: understand the problem, roughly scope what solving it requires, then talk about what that costs. When a client inverts this — leads with "what's your rate" or "we have $3,000, what can you do" before you've discussed what they're trying to achieve — it's usually a sign that design is being procured like a commodity rather than commissioned as a problem-solving service.

This isn't automatically disqualifying. Small businesses and early-stage founders often have genuinely fixed, hard budget ceilings and are right to mention them early so nobody wastes time. The distinction to look for is whether the client treats the number as a constraint to design around, or as the entire brief. "We have $3,000 — what can we accomplish with that toward reducing signup drop-off?" is a workable, honest constraint. "We have $3,000 — send us a Figma file with a homepage, five inner pages, a logo, and a style guide" is a shopping list dressed up as a budget, and it tells you the client is thinking in units of deliverables, not units of outcomes.

You can hear the same thing from the other direction, too. A client who asks about your day rate or project fee before asking a single question about your process, your past work in their category, or how you'd approach their specific problem is often evaluating you as an interchangeable line item. That's fine for genuinely commoditized work — icon sets, template customization, production support — but it's a poor match for strategic design work, where the value is precisely in the parts that resist being itemized in advance.

A useful gut-check question to ask yourself after a first call: did the client ask more questions about price or more questions about approach? If it's overwhelmingly price, with almost no curiosity about how you'd think through their problem, they may not yet see design as a decision-making process worth paying for — they see it as a production cost to minimize. You can sometimes shift that perception with a strong case study or a short paid discovery engagement that demonstrates the value of the thinking, not just the pixels. But if the budget-first framing persists after that, it's a genuine signal, not a misunderstanding, and it's worth respecting rather than talking yourself out of.

Sign 4: They want a Dribbble shot, not a business outcome

Some clients arrive with a folder of screenshots — beautiful, polished, often from products in entirely different industries with entirely different user bases — and the brief is essentially "make ours look like these." Inspiration references are completely normal and useful; the problem is when the reference is the entire strategy, with no translation layer for the client's actual users, actual content, or actual business constraints.

This sign is closely related to Sign 1, but it deserves its own entry because it comes from a different place: not an absence of thinking about the problem, but an active substitution of aesthetic goals for functional ones. The tell is in the vocabulary. A client oriented toward outcomes talks about conversion, retention, task completion, support ticket volume, or time-to-value. A client oriented toward appearance talks about "feel," "vibe," "premium," "modern," and — the most common one — "clean." None of those words are wrong to use, but when they're the only words a client uses to describe success, there's no way to know, after delivery, whether the design actually worked, only whether it was liked.

The practical risk here is specific: aesthetic-only briefs are the ones most prone to endless subjective revision, because there's no objective standard to close the loop. If the goal is "reduce checkout abandonment by simplifying the form," you can look at a design and ask whether it does that. If the goal is "make it feel premium," there is no design in the world that can be definitively confirmed to feel premium, because the standard lives entirely in the client's head and can shift every time they look at it.

"I'll know it when I see it."

That phrase, however politely delivered, is a request to design blind, round after round, until you happen to guess correctly. It's worth naming this pattern directly and proposing an alternative early: translate the aesthetic references into a short list of concrete attributes ("generous whitespace, muted palette, serif headlines, minimal iconography") that can actually be checked against, and — more importantly — tie at least one or two success measures to something outside pure taste, even something soft like "the client's internal team can navigate to any page in under three clicks" or "new users understand the value proposition within five seconds of landing." Outcomes don't have to be hardcore analytics to be useful; they just have to be checkable by someone other than the client's mood that day.

If a client resists defining any success measure beyond "we'll know it when we see it," treat that as a genuine readiness gap rather than a personality quirk. It usually means the internal conversation about what the product or brand is actually trying to do hasn't happened yet, and design is being asked to stand in for strategy work it can't substitute for.

Sign 5: Every deadline is "yesterday," and nothing else moves

Urgency itself isn't a warning sign — plenty of legitimate projects have real, externally imposed deadlines: a trade show, an investor demo, a regulatory date. The warning sign is asymmetric urgency: the client needs the deliverable immediately, but everything upstream of the deliverable — content, decisions, approvals, access to stakeholders — moves at a completely different, much slower pace.

This shows up in a familiar shape. The client insists the project must launch in three weeks. You ask for final copy, brand assets, or a decision-maker's calendar availability, and suddenly the response time stretches to days. Feedback rounds that were supposed to take 48 hours take a week and a half. Meanwhile the original deadline never moves, and somehow the compressed time gets absorbed entirely into your side of the process — more revision cycles jammed into less time, more "can we get this by tomorrow morning" requests as the original date approaches.

This pattern is a readiness issue because it usually reveals that the deadline is not actually connected to a real external event — it's a symptom of the project having been delayed internally for a long time before you were ever contacted, with your engagement now expected to compress not just the design work but also all of the client's own unfinished internal groundwork. You are, in effect, being asked to absorb someone else's procrastination as your own emergency.

A few concrete questions during scoping can surface this before it becomes your problem:

  • "What happens on [the deadline date] specifically — is there an external event, or is the date more of a target?" Vague answers here are informative.
  • "Who owns getting me the content and assets, and when will they be ready?" If nobody has a clear answer, the timeline is fictional regardless of what's written in the contract.
  • "What was happening with this project before you reached out to me?" A project that's been in internal limbo for six months rarely turns crisp and fast the moment a designer joins.

When the asymmetry is real but the client is otherwise a good fit, the solution isn't to refuse the deadline outright — it's to make the dependency explicit and contractual. State plainly that the delivery date holds only if content and feedback arrive by specific interim dates, and that missed interim dates shift the final date correspondingly. Clients who are genuinely ready to work with a designer accept this kind of structure without much friction, because they understand a deadline is a shared commitment, not something imposed unilaterally on the person doing the work. Clients who push back hard on that structure — who want the deadline locked but refuse to commit to the dependencies that make it achievable — are telling you, quite clearly, that they expect the compression to land entirely on you.

Turning a readiness gap into a real project

Spotting these signs isn't primarily about deciding whether to say no — though sometimes that's the right call. More often, it's about deciding what kind of "yes" makes sense. A client who can't articulate the problem, has no clear decision-maker, leads with budget, wants a look rather than an outcome, and has an unrealistic deadline is not a client to walk away from reflexively. They're a client who needs a different scope than the one they originally asked for.

A few practical moves consistently help here:

  1. Sell a discovery phase, not a leap into production. If the problem isn't defined, propose a short, separately priced phase — stakeholder interviews, a review of existing data, a written problem statement and success criteria — before any design work begins. This does two things at once: it fixes Sign 1 directly, and it gives you a low-risk way to test whether the client is willing to invest in getting the problem right, which is itself diagnostic.
  2. Ask for a named decision-maker in writing before kickoff. Not as a bureaucratic formality, but as a genuine process requirement. Frame it as being in the client's interest too: "This keeps us from redoing work because of conflicting feedback, and keeps your team's time efficient." Most reasonable clients will designate someone once they understand why it matters.
  3. Reframe budget conversations around scope, not discounting. If a client's budget is fixed and real, don't quietly shrink the quality of the work to fit it — shrink the scope. A smaller, well-defined engagement done properly builds more trust than a full engagement done under-resourced.
  4. Translate aesthetic references into checkable criteria and at least one real success measure. Even a soft, qualitative measure gives both sides something to point to besides personal taste when a revision debate starts going in circles.
  5. Make timeline dependencies explicit and mutual. A deadline with named upstream dependencies and consequences for missed interim dates isn't adversarial — it's the version of "we're in this together" that actually holds up under pressure.

It's worth saying plainly that not every readiness gap is fixable in a single proposal email, and that's fine. Some clients need an internal alignment meeting before they're ready to talk to you at all — and telling them so, kindly and specifically, is more useful to them than a polished mockup produced against a brief that was never solid to begin with. Turning down or postponing a project because the client isn't ready yet is not a failure of business development; it's often the only version of the engagement that would have actually worked. The alternative — starting anyway, and hoping structure and clarity show up somewhere around round four — is where most of the horror stories in this industry actually come from.

The goal of watching for these five signs isn't to become suspicious of every new client conversation. It's to get better at telling the difference between a client who needs a designer's judgment and a client who needs, first, a conversation that has nothing to do with design at all — and to have the confidence to offer that conversation instead of quietly designing around the gap and hoping nobody notices.
